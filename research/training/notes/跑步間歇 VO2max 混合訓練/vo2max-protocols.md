# VO2max protocols for a recreationally active non-runner man in his 30s (research note, 2026-11-22)

Tags: [V] = I read the PubMed abstract myself this session (via NCBI E-utilities). [U] = not verified; background knowledge only, do not rely on it.
PubMed's web UI blocked automated fetches (cookie wall), so everything [V] is abstract-level, not full text. Numbers below are copied from the abstracts.

## 0. Evidence gaps (stated up front)
- **Incline walking / rower vs running as the HIIT mode: no verified study found.** Search returned nothing usable. Status: [U]. See section 2.
- **"4 min run / 3 min walk" as a specific protocol**: it is the Helgerud 2007 4x4 structure (4 min work, 3 min active recovery at ~70 % HRmax) [V]; no trial of a *walk-run* version in non-runners was found.
- **Hep B + HIIT**: no study found. No ACSM text on Hep B read. Safety section is therefore about cardiac screening only.
- **Submaximal-HR-at-fixed-speed and HR-recovery as progress markers**: the two papers I opened (Lamberts 2009, well-trained cyclists, n=14) are not a validation in non-runners. Weak/indirect.
- Full texts not read; only abstracts.

## 1. HIIT vs SIT vs continuous: VO2max gains

| Study | Design / n / population | Measure | Result | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Milanović 2015, Sports Med, PMID 26243014 | Meta-analysis, 28 controlled trials, n=723, healthy 18-45 y (mean 25 y, baseline 40.8 ml/kg/min) | VO2max (lab) | Endurance training vs no-exercise control: +4.9 ml/kg/min (+-1.4). HIT vs control: +5.5 (+-1.2). HIT vs endurance: +1.2 (+-0.9), "possibly small" benefit. Longer interventions and lower baseline fitness gave more. Longer HIT repetitions favoured HIT over endurance by +2.2 (+-2.1). | [V] |
| Bacon 2013, PLoS One, PMID 24066036 | Meta-analysis, 37 studies / 40 groups, n=334 (120 women), healthy sedentary or recreationally active <45 y, 6-13 wk, >=3 d/wk | VO2max | Mean +0.51 L/min (95 % CI 0.43-0.60). | Wen 2019, J Sci Med Sport, PMID 30733142 | Meta-analysis of 53 RCTs, healthy / overweight / athletic adults, searched to Apr 2018 | VO2max/peak, SMD | Vs control, even short intervals (<=30 s), low volume (<=5 min), <=4 wk gave SMD 0.79-1.65. Peripheral changes (citrate synthase +21 % HIIT, capillary density with SIT). | [V] |

Reading of the above:
- Gains of ~+3.5 to +5.5 ml/kg/min over 6-12 wk are typical in healthy younger adults (Milanović, Lock). Magnitude depends on starting fitness; fitter people gain less (Milanović: lower baseline gave +0.8-3.2 more).
- HIIT beats MICT by only ~1.2 ml/kg/min on average (Milanović) and **only if the intervals are long (>=2 min) and the volume >=15 min** (Wen). Short/SIT protocols match MICT but are not better (Wen; Bonafiglia, low quality).
- Long intervals (3-4 min) have the strongest evidence for a large central (stroke volume) adaptation (Helgerud; Bacon subset; Rosenblat).
- **Time to see gains**: HIIT trials of <=4 wk give smaller effects than >4 wk (Lock, Wen). Practical expectation: measurable at 4 wk, most gain by 8-12 wk. Milanović: longer duration added about +1.7 to +3.0.
- **Once-weekly HIIT**: only one directly relevant RCT found, Zhang 2026, Front Physiol, PMID 42534295 [V]: n=45 physically active male students, 4x4 min HIIT 1x, 2x or 3x/wk for 6 wk, lab VO2max. **1x/wk gave "only a small increase"; 2x and 3x gave marked, comparable gains**; completion 100/93/80 %. n=15 per group, 6 wk, single site, homogeneous sample, abstract gives no numbers. Weak but it is the only direct test, and it argues that one session a week is not enough to be reliable; 2/wk looks like the minimum effective dose. A children's trial (Gao 2026, PMID 42111159) found 1x/wk HIIT improved a field test; it is a different population (n=60 obese children), not applicable.
- **Whether "one HIIT/wk + easy cardio" is enough: unproven.** No adult trial found testing it. [no evidence]

## 2. Does it have to be running?
- No verified evidence on incline treadmill walking, rower, or cycling vs running for the HIIT stimulus in non-runners. A search for modality comparisons returned nothing relevant.
- What is verified: the HIIT studies that produced gains used cycling (Gillen 2016, SIT/MICT cycle) as well as running (Helgerud 2007). Both raised VO2max; they were not compared head-to-head here. The Wilson 2012 concurrent-training meta-analysis [V, PMID 22002517; 21 studies, 422 effect sizes] reports that adding **running, but not cycling**, to resistance training caused significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength, and that interference scaled with endurance frequency (r -0.26 to -0.35) and duration (r -0.29 to -0.75). Mechanism of "running is worse" is unclear from the abstract (eccentric load is a hypothesis, [U]).
- The one thing the Helgerud protocol requires is the heart-rate target (90-95 % HRmax in the last 1-2 min of each 4-min bout, 70 % in recovery). Whether a given non-running mode can reach that HR is an empirical question for Darren's own watch, not something the literature gave me. Practical test: do a 4-min bout on steep incline walking/rower/bike and read the HR; if it reaches ~90 % of HRmax by minute 2-3 it qualifies (inference, [U]).
- Walk-run intervals: no trial. The structure 4 min hard / 3 min easy is the same as Helgerud; "easy" walking at 70 % HRmax is exactly what the original protocol used (active rest), so the walking part is on-protocol. The risk is the "hard" part being jogging at too low an effort, i.e. below 90 % HRmax.

## 3. Setting intensity without a lab
- **Tanaka 2001**, J Am Coll Cardiol, PMID 11153730 [V]: HRmax = 208 - 0.7 x age. Meta-analysis of 351 studies / 18,712 subjects, validated in 514 healthy subjects (209 - 0.7 x age); independent of sex and habitual activity. Darren (36): 208 - 25.2 = **~183 bpm**.
- **Nes 2013 HUNT**, Scand J Med Sci Sports, PMID 22376273 [V]: HRmax = 211 - 0.64 x age, n=3320 with verified max effort, **SEE 10.8 bpm**. Darren: **~188 bpm**. Notes older formulas underestimate HRmax in people over 30.
- Both give 183-188; individual error is +-11 bpm (Nes SEE), so a formula is only a start. Using 183: 90-95 % = 165-174 bpm; 70 % = 128. Using 188: 169-179; 132.
- **Field-test HRmax**: the best way is to take the highest HR seen at the end of a hard 4-min interval or all-out hill/treadmill effort (inference; I found no paper that I read on the field-test approach). Garmin's own max HR setting is a guess unless he has hit it. Treat the highest HR repeatedly seen as HRmax.
- **Talk test**: Persinger 2004, MSSE, PMID 15354048 [V], n=16 healthy, treadmill and cycle: the point where speech first becomes difficult sat on the ventilatory threshold (~77-83 % VO2peak, ~86-89 % HRpeak treadmill). Foster 2008, PMID 18277826 [V], healthy young adults: the Talk Test tracked VT after training and after blood donation; pass-bias above VT. **It identifies moderate/threshold intensity, not the 90-95 % HRmax of a 4x4**; for the hard bouts use HR and "can only say a word or two" (RPE ~8-9/10 by inference [U]).
- RPE: no verified paper read this session on RPE 8-9 = 90-95 % HRmax. [U]

## 4. Measuring progress without a VO2max reading
- **Cooper 12-min run / 1.5-mile**: McNaughton 1998, Percept Mot Skills, PMID 9842605 [V], n=32 young men (VO2max 57.9), lab treadmill VO2max: 12-min run r=0.87, 1.5-mile r=0.87, 20 m shuttle r=0.82, submax treadmill jogging test r=0.50. Bandyopadhyay 2015, Biol Sport, PMID 25729151 [V], n=88 sedentary Indian male students: r=0.93 but the standard Cooper equation **overestimated** VO2max by ~3 ml/kg/min (42.8 vs 39.8; limits 0.10-5.94). Lesson: good for tracking *change* in distance, not for absolute VO2max. A change of a few ml/kg/min is within the equation error.
- **Rockport walk**: Papini 2019, J Sports Med Phys Fitness, PMID 31215197 [V], n=30 (age 31 +- 8), 4-wk training RCT: the original Rockport equation overestimated VO2max by 22 ml/kg/min and was **not sensitive to training changes** (estimated -1.1 vs actual +1.61); authors cite cardiovascular drift. Do not use.
- **Submax HR at fixed treadmill speed/grade, HR recovery**: no validation in non-runners found; Lamberts 2009, Eur J Appl Physiol, PMID 19101720 [V partial abstract], n=14 well-trained cyclists: HR recovery improved after 8 high-intensity sessions in 4 wk. Plausible tracking marker, unvalidated against VO2max for Darren's situation. Garmin: the watch does not report VO2max for him, so its own estimates are unavailable.
- Practical: pick 1-2 repeatable tests (e.g. a 12-min flat treadmill max-distance test; a 6-8 min fixed speed/grade with average and end HR) done at the same time of day, same shoes, every 4 weeks, and judge by trend, not absolute.

## 5. Why VO2max matters (brief)
- Kodama 2009, JAMA, PMID 19454641 [V]: meta-analysis, 33 cohort studies, 102,980 participants (6,910 deaths). Each +1 MET (3.5 ml/kg/min) = all-cause mortality RR 0.87 (0.84-0.90), CHD/CVD RR 0.85. Low (<7.9 METs) vs high (>=10.9) CRF: RR 1.70.
- Mandsager 2018, JAMA Netw Open, PMID 30646252 [V]: retrospective cohort, n=122,007 treadmill-test patients (median 8.4 y). Elite vs low fitness: HR 0.20; low fitness mortality risk comparable to or greater than smoking, diabetes, CAD. Patients referred for testing, so not a healthy sample.
- Kokkinos 2022, J Am Coll Cardiol, PMID 35926933 [V]: n=750,302 US veterans (mean 61 y), median 10.2 y; lowest risk ~14 METs, 4-fold higher risk in the least fit, no upper-limit harm.
- Ross 2016 AHA statement, Circulation, PMID 27881567 [V]: CRF a potentially stronger mortality predictor than smoking, hypertension, cholesterol, T2DM.
- All observational: fitness is associated with lower mortality; none proves that raising VO2max by training lowers mortality at this age. Veteran/clinic samples are older and mostly male.

## 6. Safety
- Rognmo 2012, Circulation, PMID 22879367 [V]: 4,846 coronary heart disease patients in Norwegian rehab, 175,820 exercise hours: 1 fatal arrest per 129,456 h moderate exercise; 2 nonfatal arrests in 46,364 h of HIIT = 1 per 23,182 h. Rare, but the rate per hour was higher with HIIT (very few events; patients with known CHD, which Darren is not).
- No evidence found on HIIT in controlled chronic Hep B on entecavir (nothing relevant retrieved). Absence of evidence; his liver-related questions go to his doctor per safety-limits.
- Riebe 2015 ACSM, MSSE, PMID 26473759 [V, abstract only, text cut off]: ACSM's updated pre-participation screening model, built on (1) current activity level, (2) known cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease, (3) symptoms. The abstract says exercise is safe for most and risk falls as people become fitter. For someone already exercising regularly with no known CV/metabolic/renal disease and no symptoms, the model (from my recall [U]) does not require medical clearance to continue, including vigorous intensity. Read the full text before relying on this.
- Garber 2011 ACSM position stand, PMID 21694556 [V, abstract only]: general prescription. Warm-up/cool-down specifics not read; the Helgerud and Gillen protocols included a warm-up (Gillen: 2 min warm-up + 3 min cool-down at 50 W; Helgerud: 10 min warm-up at 70 % HRmax [U]).
- Stop for chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness, palpitations: standard ACSM warning signs ([U]).

## 7. Interference with strength (he must keep muscle and strength)
- Wilson 2012 [V] (above): concurrent training lowered hypertrophy/strength gains mainly with running and higher frequency/duration; cycling not significantly.
- Conceição 2026, J Appl Physiol, PMID 41369592 [V partial]: RCT, n=19 untrained young men, 16 wk concurrent training with long-interval HIIT vs resistance training alone: no group difference in muscle protein synthesis; type II fibre size increased; **strength gains were greater in the resistance-only group** (abstract cut off before the numbers). Small, untrained, not recreational lifters.
- Ferraro-Farro 2026, Int J Sports Med, PMID 41734815 [V]: meta-analysis of 9 RCTs (n=177), adults: SIT + resistance training vs resistance alone: no difference in lower-body strength (SMD 0.01), upper-body strength (-0.06), jump or sprint; VO2max better (SMD 0.78, p=0.001).
- Conclusion: modest HIIT volume (1-2 sessions) next to 4 lifting days did not blunt strength in these small studies; the evidence for lifters in a calorie-maintenance state is thin. Total load and recovery are the unquantified risk.

## Bottom line for Darren

1. **Long-interval HIIT (4 x 4 min at ~90-95 % HRmax, 3 min easy between) is the best-supported way to raise VO2max** (Helgerud 2007: +7 % in 8 wk at 3x/wk; Bacon 2013 and Wen 2019 favour long intervals). Expect roughly **+3 to +5 ml/kg/min (~+10 %)** in 8-12 weeks (Milanović, Lock) for a typical healthy adult; as a recreationally trained 36-year-old with ~30 min easy walking he is probably nearer the lower-fitness end where gains are larger, but that is inference.
2. **Frequency: 2 sessions a week is the best-supported minimum**; 1/wk gave only a small gain in the only direct RCT (Zhang 2026, n=15 per group, weak). 1 HIIT + easy walking is unproven for VO2max; it is fine as a start but do not expect the numbers above.
3. **Your "4 min run / 3 min walk" idea is the Helgerud structure**, so it is evidence-based IF the 4-min bout hits ~90 % HRmax (about **165-175 bpm** using Tanaka 183 / Nes 188; verify with your own highest HR) and the walk brings HR to ~130. Jogging comfortably at 75-80 % HRmax is closer to the non-effective arms of that trial.
4. **Mode**: I found no verified study showing steep incline walking, rower or bike gives equal gains, and none showing they don't. Pick the mode in which you reach the target HR within 2 min and which you can do without extra leg soreness before lifting days. Cycling showed less interference with lifting in Wilson 2012, which is a reason to favour bike or rower for the second weekly session; running is the mode flagged for strength interference.
5. **Fit within 50 min**: warm-up 8-10 min, 4 x 4 min with 3 min easy (=25 min), cool-down 5 min is about 40 min. Shorter equivalent: SIT/15-15 give similar VO2max to MICT on average but the evidence is low quality (Bonafiglia) and short intervals did not beat MICT (Wen).
6. **Measure**: every ~4 weeks, same day-type, same treadmill: a 12-min max-distance test (Cooper; r ~0.87 vs lab in young men, but equation overestimates ~3 ml/kg/min, so track distance, not the converted VO2max) and a fixed-speed submax HR. Do not use the Rockport walk.
7. **Safety**: no cardiac disease; ACSM screening for already-active symptom-free people is lenient (read full text). Hep B: no evidence either way; tell the hepatologist about the plan at the next visit as normal practice (his safety-limits memory already covers liver-related questions).
8. Strength: with 4 lifting days, keep HIIT to 2 sessions, avoid placing it before lower-body sessions; watch lifts for 4 weeks, and drop to 1 session if squat/deadlift numbers fall (a monitoring rule, not evidence-based).

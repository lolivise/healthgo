# Evidence-based prevention and reduction of chronic low-grade inflammation (diet, exercise, sleep, stress, alcohol, weight loss, supplements)

Scope note for the report writer: researched 2026-10-25. "Verified" below means the paper was located online at the linked URL (PubMed, PMC, publisher or journal page) and the stated figures came from its abstract or an indexed summary. Full texts were NOT read for most items (PubMed and Springer blocked direct fetching), so effect sizes are abstract-level. Items marked **[unverified]** were seen only as citations inside other papers. Older landmark studies are labelled **[landmark, pre-2023]**. Unit warning: some papers report CRP in mg/dL and some in mg/L (1 mg/dL = 10 mg/L); figures are given as the source states them.

General caveat that applies to every section: most diet-inflammation evidence on IL-6/TNF-α is heterogeneous (I² often > 70%), many trials are short (< 12 weeks), and effects are larger in people with high baseline CRP or existing disease. A lean-ish, very active 36-year-old with probably normal baseline CRP should expect small absolute changes from any single lever ("floor effect").

## 1. Dietary patterns and specific foods: what measurably lowers CRP / IL-6 / TNF-α?

### Takeaway
The best RCT evidence is for a Mediterranean-style pattern (modest falls in hs-CRP and IL-6, not TNF-α), marine omega-3 at roughly 1–1.2 g/day EPA+DHA in cardiometabolic populations, and avoiding excess red meat; whole grains, coffee and ultra-processed-food findings are mixed or observational only. Linoleic acid (omega-6) does not raise CRP in RCTs at normal intakes.

### Cited Findings

**Mediterranean diet (MD)**
- Keshani et al., *Nutrition Reviews* (online Nov 2025): meta-analysis of 33 RCTs, 3,476 adults. MD significantly lowered **hs-CRP, IL-6 and IL-17** vs control diets; **no significant effect on plain CRP, IL-10, TNF-α or total antioxidant capacity**. IL-6 reduction strongest in < 60 y, trials < 12 weeks and people with CVD. Authors call for higher-quality trials. — [PubMed 41211687](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41211687/); [Nutrition Reviews](https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/advance-article-abstract/doi/10.1093/nutrit/nuaf213/8317880)
- MD supplemented with olive oils (2025 meta-analysis of RCTs): CRP **SMD −0.51 (95% CI −0.96 to −0.05)**, IL-6 **SMD −0.74 (−1.16 to −0.32)**. — [PMC12105412](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12105412/)
- Older dietary-pattern RCT meta-analysis (Advances in Nutrition, ~2022): Mediterranean-type diets reduced most biomarkers (IL-6, IL-1β, CRP, IL-8, TNF-α), but the CRP effect attenuated toward null in a sensitivity analysis. — [ScienceDirect S2161831322005312](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2161831322005312)

**Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII)**
- No 2023–2026 DII umbrella review was retrieved in this session. DII evidence is almost entirely observational (scores built from food-frequency questionnaires correlated with CRP/outcomes), so it cannot establish that changing the score lowers CRP. — see Gaps.

**Fibre and whole grains** (mixed)
- 9-trial meta-analysis (Medicine, 2018) **[landmark, pre-2023]**: whole-grain diets lowered CRP (SMD 0.29, 95% CI 0.08–0.50) and IL-6 (SMD 0.19, 0.03–0.36). — [PMC6221555](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6221555/)
- JACN 2018 RCT meta-analysis **[pre-2023]**: hs-CRP Hedges' g −0.22 (−0.45 to 0.00, borderline), IL-6 lowered, TNF-α unchanged; very high heterogeneity (I² 69% CRP, 96.6% IL-6). — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07315724.2018.1490935)
- 2019 comprehensive meta-analysis found **no significant effect** of whole grains on CRP, IL-6, TNF-α or PAI-1 overall; a published commentary called an earlier positive meta-analysis "misleading". — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334454016_The_Effect_of_Whole-Grain_Intake_on_Biomarkers_of_Subclinical_Inflammation_A_Comprehensive_Meta-analysis_of_Randomized_Controlled_Trials); [PubMed 30856067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30856067/)
- Nutrients 2022 systematic review of whole-grain RCTs: inconsistent effects on inflammatory markers. — [MDPI Nutrients 14(2):374](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/2/374)

**Red and processed meat**
- 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (*Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr*): higher red meat intake raised CRP in RCTs (**N = 18, WMD +0.23 mg/L, 95% CI 0.08–0.39**); significant in people with diagnosed disease (+0.20 mg/L), **not significant in people without disease**. — [Tandfonline 10.1080/10408398.2025.2584482](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10408398.2025.2584482)
- 2022 RCT meta-analysis: smaller effect, +0.12 mg/L (0.04–0.19). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361295737_The_Effects_of_Red_Meat_Intake_on_Inflammation_Biomarkers_in_Humans_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis_of_Randomized_Controlled_Trials)

**Ultra-processed food (UPF)** (observational only)
- NHANES analysis (*Am J Med* 2025): 60–79% of energy from UPF vs 0–19% → hs-CRP RR 1.11 (1.01–1.21). — [PubMed 40912444](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40912444/)
- ELSA-Brasil cross-sectional: UPF–CRP association only in women and lost significance after adjusting for BMI (i.e., partly mediated by adiposity). — [PMC9721234](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9721234/)
- Scoping review (2025): 24 studies, CRP measured in 21; no RCTs on UPF and inflammatory markers identified. — [PMC12472508](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12472508/)

**Coffee** (observational only)
- 2024 dose-response meta-analysis of 11 cross-sectional studies (~66,000 people, search to June 2023): linear inverse association between coffee intake and CRP; modified by sex and smoking adjustment. — [PubMed 39179502](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39179502/)
- UK Biobank + Rotterdam (Clin Nutr 2023): higher habitual coffee intake associated with lower CRP; CRP partly mediated coffee's inverse association with T2D. — [Clinical Nutrition S0261-5614(23)00066-3](https://www.clinicalnutritionjournal.com/article/S0261-5614(23)00066-3/fulltext)
- Older 2020 meta-analysis found mixed results across study types. — [PMC7285227](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7285227/)

**Omega-3: dose needed to lower CRP**
- 2025 dose-response meta-analysis of **40 RCTs** in cardiometabolic disorders (*Inflammopharmacology*): **non-linear CRP reduction up to ~1,200 mg/day EPA+DHA**, in CVD, metabolic syndrome and hypertension populations; **no significant CRP reduction in overweight/obese participants**; in T2D pooled CRP fell but no dose-response. — [PubMed 40263171](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40263171/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10787-025-01744-8) (abstract-level; full text paywalled)
- Meta-analysis of omega-3/omega-6 RCTs (PMC13185561): **overall no significant effect** of omega-3/6 supplementation on IL-6, CRP or TNF-α. — [PMC13185561](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13185561/)
- 2026 FASEB J meta-analysis in sport/recovery: reports CRP and IL-6 reductions (larger IL-6 effect at ≥ 2 g/day), but the document is internally inconsistent (GRADE table lists CRP "data not available"; dose unit apparently mis-stated). Treat with caution. — [FASEB J 10.1096/fj.202504783R](https://faseb.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1096/fj.202504783R)
- Food vs supplement: no head-to-head RCT meta-analysis on CRP was found in this session — see Gaps.

**Omega-6 / linoleic acid (LA)**
- RCT meta-analysis (16 studies): increasing dietary LA did **not** significantly change CRP (SMD 0.09, −0.05 to 0.24); meta-regression hinted that very large LA increases might raise CRP. **[pre-2023]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318748437_Dietary_linoleic_acid_intake_and_blood_inflammatory_markers_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_randomized_controlled_trials)
- Systematic review of 15 RCTs in healthy adults: no evidence LA raises inflammatory markers. **[pre-2023]** — [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/81400668/Effect_of_dietary_linoleic_acid_on_markers_of_inflammation_in_healthy_persons_a_systematic_review_of_randomized_controlled_trials)
- Contrast: **conjugated** linoleic acid (CLA, a supplement, not dietary LA) raised CRP (2023 Frontiers dose-response meta-analysis, 42 studies: WMD +0.26 mg/dL, p = 0.048). — [Frontiers Immunol 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2023.1092077/full); [PMC9992184](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9992184/)
- ALA (plant omega-3) supplementation: no beneficial effect on TNF, IL-6, CRP (2017 meta-analysis of 25 trials). **[pre-2023]** — [Eur J Nutr](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00394-017-1386-2)
- No 2023–2026 dietary-LA RCT meta-analysis was found in this session.

**Fermented foods**
- Wastyk et al., *Cell* 2021 **[landmark, pre-2023]**: 36 healthy adults randomised 10 weeks to high-fermented-food (~6.3 servings/day by end) or high-fibre diet. Fermented arm: microbiome diversity rose and **19 of 93 inflammatory proteins fell (incl. IL-6)**; high-fibre arm: no diversity increase, more glycan-degrading enzymes, immune response heterogeneous. — [Stanford Medicine news](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation.html); [bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.09.30.321448v2)
- 2025 citizen-science RCT (medRxiv **preprint, not peer reviewed**) did not replicate the reduction in inflammatory proteins; the high-fibre-plus-fermented arm **increased** some immune markers (CD5, CD6, CD8A, IL-18R1). — [medRxiv 2025.08.05.25332853](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.05.25332853v1.full)
- Half a cup/day fermented vegetables for 2 months did not change inflammation markers or gut diversity in adults at CVD risk. — [PMC11404656](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11404656/)

**Extra-virgin olive oil**: only evidence found is within the MD-plus-olive-oil meta-analysis above ([PMC12105412](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12105412/)); doses in PREDIMED-type trials are far above 4 g/day (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- His pattern already scores well on the factors with the best RCT support: very little UPF, very little red meat (chicken is the main protein), high vegetables, nuts (walnuts), fish oil, yogurt (one fermented food), coffee. The main departures from a Mediterranean template are: very low olive oil (~4 g vs ~40–50 g/day in PREDIMED-style diets), little fruit, no legumes, no oily fish as food, and a single grain source (white rice + oats).
- His 1.2 g/day EPA+DHA sits right at the ~1.2 g plateau of the 2025 dose-response curve, but that curve was in cardiometabolic patients; the same paper found no CRP benefit in overweight/obese groups, and a broader meta-analysis was null overall. So the fish oil is unlikely to be doing much measurable to CRP in a healthy person. Not a reason to change it (no new supplements anyway).
- Swapping some white rice for higher-fibre carbohydrate (e.g., part legumes, brown/mixed rice) is consistent with the evidence direction but the RCT effect on CRP is uncertain/small.
- Omega-6 worry (seed oils) is not supported by RCTs at normal intakes; not a priority to audit.

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 DII umbrella review retrieved; DII evidence should be described as observational.
- No RCT meta-analysis comparing omega-3 from fish (food) vs capsules on CRP was found.
- No 2023–2026 RCT meta-analysis on legumes, nuts, fruit/polyphenols, or EVOO dose specifically on CRP was retrieved in this session (budget limit) — these exist in older literature but were not verified.
- [unverified] Wang et al. 2023 *J Am Heart Assoc* continuous dose-response omega-3 meta-analysis (seen only as a citation; its CRP findings not confirmed).
- No evidence found on protein source (chicken vs plant) and CRP beyond the red-meat comparisons.

## 2. Is a very repetitive, low-variety diet a risk for the microbiome or inflammation?

### Takeaway
The "30 plants per week" idea comes from an observational, self-reported association in the American Gut Project (McDonald et al. 2018); there is no RCT showing that dietary variety per se lowers CRP/IL-6. The variety evidence is weak; fibre amount and fermented foods have somewhat better (still small, mixed) trial evidence.

### Cited Findings
- American Gut Project (McDonald et al., *mSystems* 2018) **[landmark, pre-2023]**: people reporting > 30 plant types/week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those reporting ≤ 10, regardless of diet label (vegan, omnivore etc.), plus fewer antibiotic-resistance genes; based on self-reported food frequency, so associational. — [University of Illinois summary](https://hdh.fshn.illinois.edu/2018/05/16/illinois-scientists-highlight-impact-of-diet-in-worlds-largest-citizen-science-microbiome-project/); [ScienceAlert explainer on the 30-plants claim](https://www.sciencealert.com/eating-30-plants-a-week-a-healthier-diet-or-just-a-gimmick)
- A non-peer-reviewed AI-generated reanalysis claims the plant-diversity effect on community structure is negligible and does not generalise across countries — **low-quality source, mention only as a caution**. — [k-dense.ai report](https://www.k-dense.ai/use-cases/plant-diversity-and-gut-microbiome-analysis)
- In the Wastyk RCT, a high-fibre diet alone did **not** raise microbiome diversity over 10 weeks, while fermented foods did. — [Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation.html)

### Inferences
- His diet probably contains ~8–12 distinct plant types per week (oats, rice, spinach, kale, carrot, chia, walnuts, coffee, olive oil, maybe one fruit). That is in the "≤ 10" group of the American Gut comparison. The link to inflammation is indirect (microbiome diversity → presumed lower inflammation), not demonstrated in trials.
- Low-effort ways to raise plant count without adding tracking chores: rotate the vegetable mix, add a legume, mixed nuts/seeds, herbs/spices, a different fruit. Evidence for benefit is weak, cost is low.
- A monotonous diet also carries a micronutrient-gap risk (e.g., little fruit → lower vitamin C/polyphenols), which is a separate argument from inflammation.

### Gaps
- No RCT of dietary diversity (plant count) with CRP/IL-6 as outcome was found.
- The original McDonald 2018 mSystems paper itself was not retrieved (only secondary summaries).

## 3. Exercise: dose-response, modality, HIIT vs moderate, and can too much training raise inflammation?

### Takeaway
Chronic exercise training lowers CRP with small-to-moderate effect sizes (SMD about −0.3 to −0.8), with less consistent effects on IL-6 and TNF-α; resistance training ~3×/week for ≥ 6–12 weeks lowers CRP in middle-aged/older adults. Acute exercise transiently raises IL-6 (a myokine, not harmful), and only extreme volumes (ultramarathon) cause large acute inflammatory spikes; there is no good evidence that his training volume chronically raises inflammation.

### Cited Findings
- Umbrella review, older adults: exercise training reduced IL-6 (−0.17, 95% CI −0.32 to −0.02), TNF-α (−0.30, −0.46 to −0.13) and CRP (−0.45, −0.61 to −0.29). — [ResearchGate umbrella review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393230863_The_effect_of_physical_interventions_in_older_adults_on_inflammatory_markers_IL-6_IL-10_CRP_TNF-a_an_umbrella_review_of_systematic_reviews_and_meta-analyses)
- 2026 Bayesian network meta-analysis, older adults (*Front Physiol*): multilevel pooled IL-6 SMD −0.31, CRP −0.44, TNF-α −0.40; aerobic best for IL-6/TNF-α, resistance best for CRP; **very low certainty**. Dose-response modelled on weekly MET-min with a quadratic term (shape not confirmed in this session). — [Frontiers in Physiology 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1837579/full)
- Resistance training meta-analysis, middle-aged and older adults (*Arch Gerontol Geriatr* 2024): reduces CRP and IL-6; TNF-α effect not significant; ~3 sessions/week for 6–12 or 16–32 weeks reduced CRP. — [ScienceDirect S0167494324002127](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167494324002127)
- "Exerkine" meta-analysis (*Biomolecules* 2025): exercise reduced CRP (SMD −0.77, −1.20 to −0.33) and TNF-α (−1.09) but **increased IL-6 (SMD +0.81)**, reflecting IL-6's role as a contraction-induced myokine; high heterogeneity. — [PMC12650156](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12650156/)
- HIIT in children/adolescents with obesity: CRP SMD −0.63 (13 studies, n = 574); no significant effect on IL-6 or TNF-α. (Population not directly comparable.) — [PMC12844070](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12844070/)
- HIIT vs MICT in young/middle-aged adults (2023 meta-analysis): HIIT ≥ MICT for waist, fat mass and VO2peak; no inflammatory outcomes reported. — [PMC10048683](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10048683/)
- Ultramarathon review (2025): large acute rises in IL-6, CRP and TNF-α after ultra-endurance events, typically resolving within days. — [PMC12250383](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12250383/)
- Overtraining: OTS has been framed as a possible systemic inflammatory process, but there are no validated biomarkers apart from unexplained underperformance. — [PMC3435910](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3435910/). Elite cross-country skiers after a season had **lower** CRP than controls. — [Springer, J Sci Sport Exerc 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42978-022-00186-w)

### Inferences
- 5 strength sessions + 220–270 min/week of treadmill walking/running is well inside the range linked to lower CRP; there is no evidence that this volume, at walking/running intensity, chronically raises inflammation. The relevant risk in a deficit is under-recovery (sleep + energy), which his Garmin HRV/RHR/Body Battery already monitors better than CRP would.
- Acute post-exercise IL-6/CRP rises mean any blood test for CRP should be taken ≥ 24–48 h after a hard session to avoid a misleading result (inferred from acute-response data; not a guideline citation).
- HIIT vs moderate: no adult evidence found that one is superior for CRP; choose by preference/recovery.

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 adult meta-analysis directly comparing HIIT vs MICT on CRP/IL-6 was found.
- Dose-response shape (is there a plateau or U-curve?) in younger adults was not found; the 2026 NMA's dose-response result was not confirmed.
- No study found of chronic CRP at recreational high volumes during an energy deficit specifically.

## 4. Sleep: does improving or extending sleep lower inflammatory markers?

### Takeaway
Short or disturbed sleep is associated with higher CRP/IL-6, and multi-night experimental sleep restriction (~4.3 h for ≥ 3 nights) raises IL-6 and CRP; but there is no meta-analysis showing that sleep extension lowers them, and CBT-I trials show CRP reductions mainly with long (≥ 16-month) follow-up. Evidence on reversal is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Irwin, Olmstead & Carroll 2016 meta-analysis **[landmark, pre-2023]** (72 studies, > 50,000 people): sleep disturbance → higher CRP (ES 0.12) and IL-6 (ES 0.20); shorter sleep duration → higher CRP (ES 0.09) not IL-6; experimental deprivation/restriction not associated with CRP, IL-6, TNF-α in that analysis. — [Biological Psychiatry](https://www.biologicalpsychiatryjournal.com/article/S0006-3223(15)00437-0/abstract)
- Ballesio et al., *J Sleep Res* 2026 updated meta-analysis of experimental sleep deprivation: **multiple nights of partial deprivation (~4.3 h for 3+ nights) significantly increased IL-6 and CRP**; a single night of partial deprivation did not significantly change IL-6; authors call results preliminary pending robust RCTs. — [Wiley jsr.70099](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jsr.70099); [PMC12856123](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12856123/)
- Irwin et al. 2014 RCT **[landmark, pre-2023]** in older adults with insomnia: CBT-I reduced odds of high CRP (> 3 mg/L) at 16 months (OR 0.26, 0.07–0.97); insomnia remission associated with lower CRP. — [University of Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-vs-tai-chi-for-late-life-insomnia-an/)
- Systematic review of 11 CBT-I RCTs (searched to June 2025): IL-6 (k = 7) and CRP (k = 5) most measured; results varied, CBT-I more consistently lowered CRP than cytokines; meta-analysis not possible due to heterogeneity. — [PubMed 42165712](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42165712/)
- Recent CBT-I RCT (*Sleep Medicine* 2026): no significant effect on CRP; authors attribute to short follow-up. — [ScienceDirect S1389945726004739](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945726004739)
- Aerobic exercise RCTs (11 trials) on sleep and inflammatory markers: findings inconsistent. — [PMC12293783](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12293783/)

### Inferences
- At 6–7 h he is not in the ~4.3 h restriction range that robustly raises IL-6/CRP, but he is at the low end of the duration range associated with higher CRP in cohorts. Sleep is plausibly the weakest lever on his inflammation profile, and also matters for muscle retention in a deficit.
- Because sleep-extension RCTs with inflammatory outcomes are essentially absent, the honest framing is "protects against a known harm" rather than "proven to lower CRP".

### Gaps
- No RCT or meta-analysis of sleep extension (in short sleepers) with CRP/IL-6 outcomes was found.
- No trial evidence found on sleep regularity (consistent timing) and inflammatory markers.

## 5. Stress: mindfulness, meditation and breathing RCTs on inflammatory markers

### Takeaway
Pooled RCTs suggest mindfulness-based interventions lower CRP and IL-6 (standardised effects roughly −0.4 to −0.7), but effects are concentrated in stressed or clinical populations and are smaller/absent in healthy, low-stress adults (floor effect).

### Cited Findings
- 2025 meta-analysis on "inflammaging" (PubMed 40679650): in RCTs, MBIs reduced TNF-α (d_ppc −0.40), IL-6 (−0.70), CRP (−0.48) and pulse-wave velocity (−0.30). — [PubMed 40679650](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40679650/)
- A 2025 meta-analysis of 61 RCTs (8,401 adults) reported MBIs improved inflammation and cellular-ageing markers vs control (seen via a summary; primary paper link not retrieved). — [Examine summary](https://examine.com/research-feed/study/0AVNj9/)
- Dunn & Dimolareva 2022 meta-analysis of 48 RCTs: reductions in CRP and IL-6 post-intervention. **[pre-2023]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357799288_The_effect_of_mindfulness-based_interventions_on_immunity-related_biomarkers_a_comprehensive_meta-analysis_of_randomised_controlled_trials)
- An earlier MBI meta-analysis (HRV + inflammatory markers) found no clear effect on inflammatory markers. **[pre-2023]** — [PMC6833066](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6833066/)
- ReSource RCT in healthy adults: contemplative training reduced low-grade inflammation only in "vulnerable" participants; authors describe a floor effect in healthy, low-stressed adults. **[pre-2023]** — [PMC6920474](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6920474/)

### Inferences
- For a healthy, physically active man, expect little measurable CRP change from meditation; its more likely value for him is indirect (sleep onset, adherence, stress eating on the trip).

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 meta-analysis specific to slow-breathing / HRV-biofeedback and CRP/IL-6 was found.
- Full texts of the 2025 meta-analyses were not read; certainty ratings unknown.

## 6. Alcohol: is any level anti-inflammatory/protective? Binge effects? Hepatitis B?

### Takeaway
2023 evidence removed the "moderate drinking is protective" basis: low-volume drinking is not associated with lower all-cause mortality once abstainer bias is corrected. A single binge acutely raises gut-derived endotoxin and acute-phase proteins. In chronic hepatitis B, risk of cirrhosis and liver cancer rises dose-dependently with alcohol (+11.5% HCC risk per 12 g/day).

### Cited Findings
- Zhao et al., *JAMA Network Open* 2023;6(3):e236185: 107 cohort studies, ~4.8 million participants. **Low-volume drinking was not associated with protection against all-cause mortality**; misclassification of former/occasional drinkers as abstainers affected 86 of 107 studies. (Correction to CIs published.) — [ACC journal scan](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/journal-scans/2023/04/05/16/04/association-between-daily-alcohol); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369690442_Association_Between_Daily_Alcohol_Intake_and_Risk_of_All-Cause_Mortality_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-analyses); [Correction PMC10170333](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10170333/)
- Bala et al., *PLOS ONE* 2014 **[landmark, pre-2023]**: in 25 healthy adults (11 men, 14 women) without alcohol-use disorder, a single vodka binge caused a rapid rise in serum endotoxin and bacterial 16S rDNA (gut translocation), a prolonged rise in acute-phase proteins, and heightened ex-vivo inflammatory cytokine responses; women had higher endotoxin. — [PubMed 24828436](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24828436/); [PMC4020790](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4020790)
- Alcohol drives liver inflammation via gut endotoxin → Kupffer cell TLR4 activation (review). — [PMC3872233](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3872233/)
- Wu et al., *J Clin Transl Hepatol* 2024: meta-analysis of 45 studies, 33,272 HBV patients. Each additional **12 g alcohol/day → +6.2% cirrhosis risk and +11.5% HCC risk**; high vs low drinkers OR 2.34 (cirrhosis) and 2.42 (HCC). Significant heterogeneity, alcohol likely under-reported. (Secondary coverage swaps the overall pooled ORs 2.61/2.27; the journal abstract is authoritative.) — [JCTH 2024-00379](https://www.xiahepublishing.com/2310-8819/JCTH-2024-00379); [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/liver-health/dose-dependent-link-seen-for-alcohol-cirrhosis-liver-cancer-in-patients-with-hep-b)

### Inferences
- For him, the Taiwan trip is the main exposure. Evidence supports: no level is protective; binges are worse than spread-out intake for acute endotoxaemia; and HBV makes the liver the organ to protect, with a dose-dependent risk. Fewer drinking days and lower per-occasion dose are the evidence-consistent framing, plus discussing with his hepatologist (per his safety limits).

### Gaps
- No 2023+ meta-analysis on alcohol dose and CRP specifically was found (the J-shaped CRP association in older cohorts is likely confounded in the same way as mortality, but this was not verified).
- No study found on binge drinking specifically in HBV carriers on biomarkers.

## 7. Weight loss: CRP reduction per kg / % lost, and does rate of loss matter?

### Takeaway
CRP falls roughly linearly with weight lost, about 0.13 mg/L per kg (Selvin 2007), with ~25–40% CRP reductions after ~10–15% weight loss in trials. No trial was found that compared fast vs slow loss on CRP; faster loss mainly costs more lean mass.

### Cited Findings
- Selvin, Paynter & Erlinger, *Arch Intern Med* 2007 **[landmark, pre-2023]**: 33 studies; mean CRP change **−0.13 mg/L per kg lost** (weighted r = 0.85 overall; lifestyle-only r = 0.30). Lifestyle interventions averaged −6.2 kg with ~−0.9 mg/L CRP; surgery −33.1 kg with ~−4.5 mg/L. — [PubMed 17210875](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17210875/); [JAMA Intern Med](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/411497); [Medscape summary](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/788977)
- CALERIE-type 2-year moderate calorie restriction in non-obese adults: 10.4% weight loss, CRP ~40% and TNF-α ~50% lower vs control, without impairing cell-mediated immunity. **[pre-2023]** — [PMC4993339](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4993339/)
- Obese postmenopausal women: 15.6% weight loss → ~32% CRP reduction. **[pre-2023]** — [Circulation (AHA)](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/hc0502.103331)
- 12-week very-low-fat energy restriction in obese women: CRP −26%, correlated with weight lost. **[pre-2023]** — [ATVB](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/pdf/10.1161/01.ATV.21.6.968)
- 3-week low-calorie programme in morbid obesity (~9.4% loss): CRP fell, TNF-α and IL-6 did not. — [J Zhejiang Univ Sci B](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1631/jzus.B1400219)
- POUNDS LOST RCT: hs-CRP fell by similar amounts regardless of diet macronutrient composition. **[pre-2023]** — [PubMed 23712970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23712970/)
- Substantial fat-mass loss reduced low-grade inflammation even in normal-weight individuals. — [Scientific Reports 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-40107-6)
- TEMPO trial protocol (fast vs slow weight loss): rationale that larger deficits may induce stronger adaptive responses and more lean-mass loss; no CRP result retrieved. — [PMC6165329](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6165329/)

### Inferences
- He has lost ~14 kg (102 → 88 kg, ~14%). Using Selvin's slope that predicts roughly −1.8 mg/L CRP if his baseline was elevated; in percentage terms trials show ~25–40% falls at this magnitude of loss. If his baseline CRP was already low, the absolute change will be smaller (floor effect). Weight loss is very likely the biggest single anti-inflammatory change he has made.
- Rate: no evidence that a faster cut lowers CRP more; the evidence-based cost of a faster rate is lean-mass loss, which is relevant to his strength goals and the "face ageing" question in this research folder.

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 meta-analysis expressing CRP change per % body weight was found.
- No trial comparing fast vs slow weight loss with CRP/IL-6 as outcomes was found (TEMPO results on inflammation not retrieved).
- Whether CRP rebounds with weight regain/overeating (e.g., a 5-week trip) was not researched.

## 8. Supplements (report only, not recommendations): curcumin, vitamin D, omega-3 capsules, and liver safety

### Takeaway
Curcumin shows moderate-certainty CRP reductions in meta-analyses (mostly in patients), vitamin D's CRP effect is inconsistent and concentrated in people with high baseline CRP, and omega-3 capsules lower CRP mainly in cardiometabolic patients. Turmeric/curcumin carries a documented, increasing signal of idiosyncratic hepatocellular liver injury (strongly linked to HLA-B*35:01), which is directly relevant to someone with hepatitis B.

### Cited Findings
- Curcumin umbrella meta-analysis (Naghsh et al. 2023, *Evid Based Complement Alternat Med*): 10 meta-analyses, ~5,870 participants; CRP ES −0.74, IL-6 −1.07, TNF-α −1.92; CRP certainty rated moderate (GRADE). — [PMC9870680](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9870680/); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2023/4875636)
- GRADE-assessed dose-response meta-analysis (*Cytokine* 2023): CRP WMD −0.58 mg/L, TNF-α −3.48 pg/mL, IL-6 reduced. — [ScienceDirect S1043466623000224](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1043466623000224)
- Critical umbrella review of curcumin (2025) and a review of meta-analyses: results mixed (CRP lower in 7/10 meta-analyses, IL-6 in 5/8; null in hemodialysis). — [PMC12176752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12176752/); [PMC11174746](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11174746/)
- Turmeric liver injury (DILIN, Halegoua-De Marzio et al., *Am J Med* 2023, online 2022): 10 cases (2004–2022); hepatocellular injury, latency 1–4 months, 1 death from acute liver failure; **HLA-B*35:01 allele frequency 0.45 vs 0.056–0.069 in controls**; 3 of 7 products contained piperine (black pepper, increases absorption); authors say turmeric liver injury appears to be increasing in the US. — [Am J Med full text](https://www.amjmed.com/article/S0002-9343(22)00740-9/fulltext); [DILIN poster](https://dilin.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/2022-DiLiN-DDW-Poster-Liver-Injury-Associated-with-Turmeric-Dina-Halegoua-De-Marzio-MD-Victor-Navarro-MD.pdf)
- Vitamin D: 2014 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (n = 924) found hs-CRP −1.08 mg/L, larger when baseline hs-CRP ≥ 5 mg/L **[pre-2023]** — [PubMed 24918698](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24918698/); 2022 umbrella meta-analysis CRP ES −0.42 — [ScienceDirect S1043661822004303](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1043661822004303); 2024 GRADE-assessed COVID-19 meta-analysis: CRP not significant overall (low certainty). — [Wiley prp2.70013](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/prp2.70013)
- Omega-3 capsules: see Section 1 (2025 dose-response meta-analysis, plateau ~1.2 g/day EPA+DHA in cardiometabolic patients; null in overweight/obese; broader meta-analysis null). — [PubMed 40263171](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40263171/); [PMC13185561](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13185561/)

### Inferences
- The turmeric/curcumin hepatotoxicity signal, combined with chronic HBV and his standing "no new supplements, doctor referral for anything liver-related" rule, makes curcumin a clear "do not add" item regardless of its CRP effect sizes. Culinary turmeric in food is a different exposure from concentrated, piperine-enhanced supplements (inference; not separately studied here).
- Vitamin D only plausibly matters if he is deficient; that is a blood-test question for his doctor, not a supplement decision.

### Gaps
- UK Committee on Toxicity turmeric statement was seen only via a blog; not verified against the COT document.
- No 2023–2026 vitamin D–CRP meta-analysis in healthy adults was found.
- No data found on curcumin hepatotoxicity specifically in HBV carriers.

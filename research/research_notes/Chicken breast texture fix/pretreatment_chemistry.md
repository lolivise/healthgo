# Pre-treatments for juicier, more tender cut chicken breast: baking soda, salt/brine, phosphates, and interaction with acidic/yoghurt marinades

Evidence grades used throughout: **[PR]** peer-reviewed (I saw the abstract or a summary of it; full texts were mostly paywalled or blocked, so only numbers quoted here were actually seen); **[ET]** expert-tested (America's Test Kitchen / Cook's Illustrated, controlled kitchen test); **[RC]** reputable recipe/cooking site practice; **[AN]** anecdotal or low-quality; **[CALC]** my own arithmetic from cited numbers (not from a source). Species flagged where not broiler chicken breast.

Access limits: seriouseats.com is blocked for the search tool, so **nothing here is attributed to Serious Eats or J. Kenji López-Alt**. Several journal pages returned 403. Treat the sections marked Gaps as real gaps.

## Mechanism and magnitude: how pH (baking soda) and salt raise water-holding capacity and tenderness; effect sizes

### Takeaway
Both work by moving the meat's proteins away from the pH where they hold least water (isoelectric point, roughly pH 5.2-5.5), and salt additionally swells/solubilises myofibrillar protein. In broiler breast, bicarbonate (with 1% salt present) lowered cook loss by about 1.8 percentage points per 0.1% bicarbonate added, up to about 0.3%, and beat phosphate in head-to-head tests. Salt alone also helps (about 6 points less cook loss in one home test at 1% salt), but salt-only can leave meat slightly firmer/chewier.

### Cited Findings
- Broiler breast, vacuum-tumbled with a constant 1.0% NaCl in the final product plus 0 to 0.5% bicarbonate (7 levels, 203 samples): meat pH rose linearly about 0.17 units per 0.1% bicarbonate; marinade uptake peaked at 0.30% bicarbonate (11.4%) and levelled off at 0.40-0.50%; cook loss fell about 1.8% (percentage points) per 0.10% bicarbonate; hardness and chewiness decreased; appearance unchanged; authors say best performance at no more than 0.3% bicarbonate. [PR, broiler breast, Petracci et al. 2014, J Poultry Sci 51(2):206-212] — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpsa/51/2/51_0130079/_article); summary also at [DOAJ search summary of same paper](https://doaj.org/article/f5f1de2c9263446e83a40eada29f7838)
- Broiler breast, six marinades of salt / phosphate (STPP) / bicarbonate and combinations plus unmarinated control: bicarbonate-containing marinades raised meat pH by about 0.7 units over control, phosphate alone or with salt only about 0.2; salt + bicarbonate gave better marinade uptake and water retention than salt + phosphate; salt + phosphate + bicarbonate performed best. Low-field NMR showed the extra water sits inside the myofibrillar spaces (not free water) and was not extra freezable water. [PR, broiler breast, Petracci et al. 2012, Poultry Sci 91(2):526-534] — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/ps/article-abstract/91/2/526/1535403); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22252368/) (via search summary)
- Chicken breast fillets marinated with 0.3% bicarbonate vs 0.3% tripolyphosphate (salt present in both), then oven-cooked at several temperature combinations: bicarbonate fillets retained more water under severe heat (67.3% vs 65.7%, P<0.05) and had lower cook loss (30.7% vs 33.4%, P<0.05); texture (TPA) of bicarbonate fillets was less sensitive to the cooking temperature than that of phosphate fillets. [PR, chicken breast, Mudalal et al. 2014, Food and Nutrition Sciences] — [Najah Univ. record](https://staff.najah.edu/en/publications/803/); [summary page](https://staff-old.najah.edu/samer/published-research/comparison-between-quality-traits-phosphate-and-bicarbonate-marinated-chick) (I could not open the full abstract directly; numbers came via search summary)
- Spent-laying-hen breast (NOT broiler; tougher meat), 300 pieces, 10 treatments (water, bicarbonate 0.10-0.25 M, lactic acid 0.05-0.20 M): bicarbonate raised and lactic acid lowered pH of raw and cooked meat; both marinades lowered cooked shear force, which authors link to higher water-holding capacity; bicarbonate gave a porous structure, lactic acid left gaps between fibres; fibres in marinated meat were wider (swelling). Suggested 0.15 M bicarbonate (about 1.3% w/v; [CALC] using 84 g/mol) or 0.10 M lactic acid. Raw values (pH, shear N, cook loss) and marination time were not in the abstract I read. [PR, spent hen breast, Koeipudsa et al. 2019, Asia-Pac J Sci Technol 24(4)] — [APST article page](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/APST/article/view/189285)
- Home test (100 g chicken breast + 100 g water, 18 h vacuum-sealed fridge): control, 0.1% STPP, 1% kosher salt, and 1% salt + 0.1% STPP, all by total weight. Cooked to 165 F: control 70 g (about 30% loss), STPP-only 70 g, salt 76 g, salt + STPP 76 g. Both salted samples tasted similar but slightly firmer/chewier, attributed to salt's effect on protein. [AN/ET-style, one cook, n=1 per arm] — [Modernist Pantry blog](https://blog.modernistpantry.com/advice/can-sodium-tripolyphosphate-improve-brining/)
- Vacuum-tumbled broiler breast fillets with 0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.25, 1.50% NaCl, all with 0.35% STP: marination improved lightness, shear force and cooking loss versus unmarinated; authors conclude 0.5-1.0% NaCl can effectively marinate broiler breast. Direction only; no numbers in abstract. [PR (M.S. thesis), broiler breast, Lopez 2011, Mississippi State] — [Scholars Junction](https://scholarsjunction.msstate.edu/td/1685)
- Poultry (chicken and turkey) dissertation: salt treatments generally raised pH, cooked yield and water-binding versus controls; breast had lower pH and lower water binding than thigh/drumstick. [PR-level dissertation, abstract only] — [Purdue](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI8300907)
- Mechanism for salt: low NaCl concentrations swell myofibres and raise water content; very high concentrations shrink fibres and precipitate protein (pork brining study); NaCl increases solubility of muscle proteins and water binding. [PR, mostly pork/beef abstracts] — [CONICET pork NaCl diffusion record](https://repositoriosdigitales.mincyt.gob.ar/vufind/Record/CONICETDig_4c213f96980d519b8c91b1702c12b965)
- Mechanism for bicarbonate: raises pH away from isoelectric point (water-holding capacity is minimal near about pH 5.2-5.5), has strong buffering (HCO3- buffers about pH 6.4-10.3) and ionic strength. [PR/review via search summaries; beef-focused paper] — [SciELO NaHCO3 beef paper](https://new.scielo.br/j/cta/a/vKsPSV3QLfdxsVwnXTrdLkf/?lang=en)
- ATK/Cook's Illustrated explanation: baking-soda soak raises pH at the meat's surface, making it harder for proteins to bond excessively, so meat stays tender and moist; the acid/base reaction "happens very quickly and does not build much over time." [ET] — [ATK how-to](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/6707-tenderizing-meat-with-a-baking-soda-solution)

### Inferences
- The strongest chicken-breast evidence (Petracci 2012/2014, Mudalal 2014) is for bicarbonate used together with salt (1% NaCl in the 2014 dose-response). The benefit is therefore probably additive or synergistic with salt, not a substitute for it. I found no clean bicarbonate-alone vs salt-alone vs both comparison in broiler breast (see Gaps).
- Magnitude to expect: a cook-loss reduction of several percentage points (about 5-6 points for salt in a home test; up to about 5 points more for 0.3% bicarbonate by linear extrapolation of 1.8 points per 0.1%) out of a roughly 30% baseline. That is meaningful but not magic. [CALC/inference, not a measured combined result]
- If his current meat is "dry and tough/chewy", overcooking in the air-fryer is likely a larger driver than pre-treatment (outside this note's scope; other researchers may cover it). Pre-treatments widen the doneness window; they do not remove it.

### Gaps
- No broiler-breast study found that tests bicarbonate alone, salt alone and both in the same design with numeric shear force (WBS) values; full texts were paywalled.
- Actual shear force (N) values for the studies above were not available in abstracts.
- Whether the benefit of surface-only (soak/rinse or dusted) bicarbonate matches the tumbled-in 0.3% bicarbonate of the studies: not studied in what I found. The 0.3% figure is bicarbonate taken up through the whole piece in a tumbled brine, a different regime from a short soak on cut pieces.

## Baking soda dose, time, rinse, failure modes; what reputable cooking sources say

### Takeaway
Cooking sources use about 3/4 tsp per 250 g (RecipeTin Eats) or 1 tsp per 12 oz/340 g (ATK) for 15-30 minutes on thin slices or small pieces, then rinse. That is roughly 1.0-1.4% of meat weight in bicarbonate, several times the 0.3% that studies call optimal for tumbled-in uptake, so the rinse is doing real work and over-time/over-dose gives mushy, bouncy or soapy results. Serious Eats / Kenji could not be checked.

### Cited Findings
- ATK/Cook's Illustrated test: 1 tsp baking soda dissolved in water per 12 oz sliced chicken (or sliced pork); 1/4 tsp for 12 oz ground beef; standard soak is 15-20 min; 45 min was indistinguishable from 15 min in their tests (ground beef kept only about 3% more moisture at 45 min than 15); they conclude 15 min is enough and slightly longer is fine. The page does not describe rinsing or any failure mode. [ET] — [ATK](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/6707-tenderizing-meat-with-a-baking-soda-solution)
- A Cook's Illustrated stir-fry pork recipe cited in a Cooking Science Guy PDF used 1 tsp baking soda in 1/2 cup water for 15 min. [secondary citation of ET; pork] — [Cooking Science Guy PDF](https://www.cookingscienceguy.com/pages/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/The-Many-Lives-and-Uses-of-Baking-Soda.pdf) (seen only via search summary)
- RecipeTin Eats: 3/4 tsp bi-carb per 250 g (8 oz) chicken breast slices or bite-size pieces, tossed dry, refrigerate 20 min (slices) or 30 min (bite-size), rinse well under running water or in a colander, shake and pat. Says method is for slices/small pieces only, NOT whole breasts or thigh fillets (surface over-tenderised before the inside is affected). Left almost an hour: "a bit more soft than I'd like". Commenter: 25 min on slices was "close to too tender"; another misread 3/4 tsp as 3-4 tsp and disliked it; others report "bouncy"/"weird" texture. Author suspects baking soda slightly shortens raw-chicken shelf life, so uses within 24 h or freezes; does not say whether to velvet before or after freezing. [RC with anecdotal comments] — [RecipeTin Eats](https://www.recipetineats.com/velveting-chicken-chinese-restaurant-tenderise-chicken/)
- Chinese Cooking Demystified: classical velveting uses about 1/4 tsp baking soda per about 200 g meat inside a cornstarch/egg-white/salt/oil marinade (no rinse), then passes through oil at about 150 C for 30-45 s. A separate restaurant soak-and-rinse method (nenhua) is for thicker/tougher cuts. Baking soda can leave an unpleasant aftertaste; author prefers sodium carbonate for neutrality. Papain can over-tenderise if left too long, unlike baking soda. Author has never done a baking soda soak and says it is not needed for most dishes. [RC] — [Chinese Cooking Demystified](https://chinesecookingdemystified.substack.com/p/a-guide-to-velveting)
- A low-quality aggregator page warns bicarbonate left on the surface causes bitter/soapy/metallic taste and suggests 1-2 min rinse; treat as unverified. [AN] — [search summary of ticketlog.com.br page](https://www.lojasouparceiro.ticketlog.com.br/celebrity-radar-news-hub-005/the-5-step-secret-to-ultra-tender-meat-how-to-use-baking-soda-for-perfect-velveting-86rf5.html)
- Another blog guide: 1/2 tsp per 1 lb (450 g) chicken, rinse thoroughly to avoid metallic taste; claims reaction "does not stop at 20 minutes." [AN] — [DishScience.blog](https://dishscience.blog/tenderize-chicken-with-baking-soda)
- Doses as % of meat weight [CALC, assuming 4.6 g per level tsp from [Soupersage/USDA-derived nutrition listing](https://www.soupersage.com/nutrition-calories-protein/baking-soda)]: RecipeTin Eats 3.45 g/250 g = 1.4%; ATK 4.6 g/340 g = 1.35%; DishScience 2.3 g/454 g = 0.5%; Chinese Cooking Demystified 1.15 g/200 g = 0.6%. Peer-reviewed optimum (tumbled, salt present): at or below 0.3%.
- Related enzyme/other: Koeipudsa 2019 and Petracci 2014 both found texture benefit from bicarbonate only up to a moderate level; Petracci 2014 marinade uptake plateaued above 0.3%, and the authors' best-performance statement is "no higher than 0.3%." [PR] — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpsa/51/2/51_0130079/_article)

### Inferences
- A conservative home dose that stays near the studied range and the lower end of cooking practice: about 0.3-0.6% of meat weight (3-6 g/kg; roughly 3/4-1.3 tsp per kg [CALC]) for 15-20 min on pieces under about 2 cm thick. This is my synthesis, not a source recommendation. Cooking sources use higher doses (about 1.4%) but rinse.
- Failure modes with strongest support: (1) over-time or over-dose on small cut pieces produces soft/bouncy/over-tenderised outer layer (RecipeTin Eats author and commenters); (2) residual bicarbonate gives soapy/metallic taste (multiple blog sources; no peer-reviewed sensory threshold found); (3) thick whole breasts do not suit a short soak because the surface changes before the centre.
- No source I could verify says "do not rinse" for the soak method; ATK's page is silent on rinsing; the classical in-marinade version is not rinsed but uses a far smaller dose (about 0.6%).

### Gaps
- Serious Eats / Kenji López-Alt and Woks of Life positions: not retrieved (Serious Eats blocked to the search tool; a guide summarised there places Serious Eats and Woks of Life in the "egg white/cornstarch" camp, which I could not confirm).
- No controlled study found on soaking/dusting cut broiler breast in 1% bicarbonate then rinsing (dose-response, time-course, sensory off-flavour threshold).
- No data found on whether bicarbonate-treated raw chicken tolerates freezing differently (RecipeTin Eats author only mentions freezing as a storage option).

## Velveting followed by an acidic (vinegar/mustard) or yoghurt marinade: neutralisation, order, bicarbonate in the marinade

### Takeaway
I found no study (and no reputable cooking source) that tests alkaline-then-acid sequencing on chicken breast, so the answer is inference from chemistry and from the separate bicarbonate and acid studies. Both treatments lower shear force in chicken separately; chemically, an acid marinade would push surface pH back down, and the rinse after velveting removes most of the bicarbonate, so stacking them is unlikely to hurt but the pH benefit is likely partly cancelled at the surface.

### Cited Findings
- In the same experiment, bicarbonate raised and lactic acid lowered pH of raw and cooked spent-hen breast; both lowered shear force vs controls. [PR, spent hen] — [APST](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/APST/article/view/189285)
- Bicarbonate has high buffering capacity (HCO3- buffers about pH 6.4-10.3), described by a review as a reason it outperforms phosphate in some measures. [PR review via search summary] — [Petracci 2010 record](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/91139) (abstract not directly readable; I saw this via a search summary)
- Water-holding capacity of meat is minimal when pH is near the myofibrillar isoelectric point (about 5.2-5.5); acid marinades that move meat toward that range are expected to reduce water retention. [PR via search summary; beef paper] — [SciELO](https://new.scielo.br/j/cta/a/vKsPSV3QLfdxsVwnXTrdLkf/?lang=en)
- ATK: the baking-soda reaction is fast and "does not build much over time", and it acts at the meat's surface. [ET] — [ATK](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/6707-tenderizing-meat-with-a-baking-soda-solution)
- Meat pH after bicarbonate marination rises linearly with dose (about 0.17 units per 0.1% bicarbonate) and bicarbonate-containing marinades lifted breast pH about 0.7 units. [PR] — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpsa/51/2/51_0130079/_article); [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/ps/article-abstract/91/2/526/1535403)

### Inferences
- Order: velvet first, rinse, then marinate is the logical order, because (a) bicarbonate dissolved directly into an acidic marinade is consumed by the acid (it reacts to CO2, water and a sodium salt of the acid), so you lose both the pH lift and the acid, and (b) the raised-pH layer is only at the surface and short-lived. This is chemistry, not a cited result.
- The net effect of a vinegar-and-mustard or yoghurt marinade after velveting is probably to pull surface pH partway back; the cooked result would still carry any texture change already made to the surface proteins during the 15-20 min soak, since protein/water binding changes persist to a degree. I found no data quantifying how much carries over. Treat as plausible, unverified.
- Adding baking soda directly into the Mustard (vinegar) or Tikka (yoghurt) marinade is a poor idea for the reasons in the first bullet; the bubbling/neutralisation wastes it and leaves unrinsable sodium. (Yoghurt is mildly acidic; I did not source its pH.)
- Since he freezes the chicken in the marinade, thaws, and discards the marinade, the marinade's contact time is long (days frozen, then thaw), so the surface pH is set mostly by the marinade, not the earlier soak. Freezing-in-marinade effects are not studied in anything I found.

### Gaps
- No study of sequential bicarbonate then acid marinade on chicken breast.
- No measured pH of Mustard (vinegar) or yoghurt marinades or of the meat after each step; would need a pH meter or strips (home measurement would answer this directly).
- Whether any bicarbonate-driven benefit survives rinse + acid marinade + freezing: no data.

## Salt alone vs baking soda alone vs both; salt level needed; comparison with his marinades; dry-brine level and time

### Takeaway
Evidence supports salt at roughly 0.5-1.0% of meat weight for broiler breast (studies use 1% with bicarbonate; 0.5-1.0% with phosphate), with bicarbonate at 0.3% or less adding further cook-loss reduction. His Mustard (about 0.38% salt-equivalent) is at or below the bottom of that range, and Tikka (about 0.77%) is in the range, but both are marinade totals that are not fully absorbed. I found no dry-brine-specific peer-reviewed numbers for chicken breast.

### Cited Findings
- Petracci 2014 used 1.0% NaCl w/w in the final product as the base for all bicarbonate levels. [PR] — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpsa/51/2/51_0130079/_article)
- Mississippi State: 0.5-1.0% NaCl (with 0.35% STP) effective for broiler breast; effect of marination improved shear force and cooking loss vs unmarinated. [PR thesis] — [Scholars Junction](https://scholarsjunction.msstate.edu/td/1685)
- Pakistani vacuum-tumbled fillet study: 0.75% NaCl + 0.45% STPP gave significantly improved shear force; 1.25% NaCl + 0.35% STPP gave significantly higher cooking yield and was most acceptable to the sensory panel. [PR, via search summary] — [JAPS](https://thejaps.org.pk/manuscript/vs-18-0067.pdf)
- Samgyetang (Korean whole-chicken soup) study: raising brine salt by 0.5 percentage points reduced cooking loss and shear force and raised moisture and water-holding capacity; sensory acceptability was highest at 2.0% brine salt. Phosphate in the brine also reduced cook loss and shear force. [PR, whole chicken in soup context, via search summary] — [PMC6411240](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6411240)
- Home test: 1% salt (of total chicken + water weight) retained about 6 percentage points more weight after cooking than water-only (76 g vs 70 g per 100 g) and tasted slightly firmer/chewier. [AN-level, n=1] — [Modernist Pantry](https://blog.modernistpantry.com/advice/can-sodium-tripolyphosphate-improve-brining/)
- Cook's Illustrated: wet brine of about 9% salt by weight of water recommended for chicken breasts; they cite studies saying meat absorbs the most moisture at about 6% salt; the 6% brine was absorbed slightly more but tasters preferred 9% for saltiness. Time not stated in the extract I read. [ET] — [ATK/CI brine how-to](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/cooksillustrated/how_tos/6428-a-better-brine)
- Dry-brine rules of thumb: salt weighed as a percentage of meat weight (1% = 10 g per kg); about 2% quoted by one BBQ site for a "common target" (that is for other uses; far too salty for his sodium budget). [AN] — [Destination BBQ](https://destination-bbq.com/brining-math-explained/)
- Comparison arithmetic [CALC]: his figures are 1,500 mg sodium/kg chicken (Mustard) = 3.8 g salt-equivalent/kg = 0.38%; 3,000 mg/kg (Tikka) = 7.6 g/kg = 0.76-0.77%. Salt 39.3% Na. A 1.0% dry brine = 10 g salt/kg = about 3,930 mg sodium/kg.
- Bicarbonate benefit above is documented in the presence of salt (1% in the 2014 study); the combination salt + bicarbonate was best or equal to salt + phosphate in the 2012 study. [PR] — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/ps/article-abstract/91/2/526/1535403)

### Inferences
- Which matters more: there is no direct head-to-head evidence in broiler breast. The best-supported statement is that salt (about 0.75-1.0%) is the foundation and bicarbonate at 0.3% or less adds on top. In the one home test, salt alone gave a measurable cook-yield gain; in the same test STPP did nothing extra.
- Mustard marinade (about 0.38% salt-equivalent, not all absorbed) is probably below the level at which studies see consistent brining benefits; it also contains acid (vinegar in the mustard). Tikka (about 0.77%) is near the lower-middle of the effective range, but the yoghurt marinade is a coating and some salt stays on the discarded marinade after thaw. He discards the marinade, so absorbed salt is less than the totals.
- A dry brine of 0.75-1.0% salt by meat weight (7.5-10 g/kg), applied to cut pieces 1-24 h before marinating or before freezing, would be in the studied range. Time is not supported by chicken-specific peer-reviewed data I found; the studies were vacuum-tumbled (minutes to hours) or 18 h soaks. Treat time as unverified; small pieces equilibrate faster than whole breasts.
- Salt can make meat slightly firmer/chewier at higher levels or if the pieces are overcooked (Modernist Pantry note). Given his complaint is "tough/chewy", do not go much above 1%.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed dry-brine (surface salt, no added water) study on chicken breast found; the 0.75-1.0% recommendation is extrapolated from tumbled/soaked studies.
- Dry-brine time courses for cut pieces: not found.
- Serious Eats/Kenji dry-brine ratios could not be retrieved.

## Do yoghurt/dairy marinades tenderise; do strong acids make breast tougher/drier/mushier

### Takeaway
For chicken breast the one controlled yoghurt study I found (acid whey, 10-15 h at 4 C) did NOT change shear force or cook loss in chicken, though it improved pork tenderness; calcium and enzyme claims are unsupported in what I found. Acid (apple cider vinegar for 48 h) lowered shear force in chicken breast in one study, so "acid makes chicken tougher" is not supported, but acid lowers pH toward the isoelectric point and can cost water-holding capacity and give mushy surfaces at strong/long exposure.

### Cited Findings
- Yoghurt acid whey (YAW) marination for 10 h and 15 h at 4 C: chicken shear force NOT affected, cooking loss NOT affected, pH reduced; but oxidative stability greatly improved. Pork (not chicken): shear force reduced after 10-15 h, cooking loss increased with YAW; at 5 h nothing changed. Paper does not discuss over-marination. Note: acid whey is the liquid by-product, not Greek yoghurt itself. [PR, chicken + pork, Karageorgou et al. 2023, Foods 12(12):2360] — [PMC10297723](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10297723)
- Food scientist Ted Russin (CIA, quoted): research is not definitive; competing theories include acid loosening the protein network and acidity helping endogenous enzymes degrade proteins. [expert opinion in popular press] — [Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/marinate-meat-in-yogurt/)
- Popular articles claim lactic acid is gentler than citrus/vinegar so meat can marinate longer without going mushy, and that calcium helps tenderise. I found no study supporting the calcium role or the specific mushiness comparison. [AN] — [Food Republic](https://www.foodrepublic.com/1488495/marinate-chicken-yogurt-tip/)
- Chicken breast marinated 48 h at 4 C in apple cider vinegar or acetic acid solutions: full-strength apple cider vinegar gave the lowest shear force; authors recommend ACV to increase tenderness; cook loss/WHC were measured but I did not see the figures. [PR, chicken breast, 2020 Turkish study, abstract via search summary] — [Selcuk Univ. record](https://acikerisim.selcuk.edu.tr/items/71bb0537-5413-4a9a-852c-2b527ce8592f)
- Lactic acid in spent-hen breast: pH fell with concentration, shear force fell vs control, but results were not monotonic with acid concentration (higher acid did not simply mean more tender). [PR, spent hen] — [APST](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/APST/article/view/189285); non-monotonic point from [search summary](https://yadda.icm.edu.pl/baztech/element/bwmeta1.element.baztech-7656a51a-d12d-4c00-81b4-d4306f6144ee/c/PTPS_2021__nr_1__s_50-55.pdf)
- Lactic acid-marinated meat showed gaps between muscle fibres (structure loosening). [PR] — [APST](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/APST/article/view/189285)
- Beef/vinegar: vinegar lowered pH and reduced cooking loss in some meat studies (species not confirmed). [PR via search summary, not chicken] — search result, no direct URL verified

### Inferences
- "Tenderising" by yoghurt in chicken breast is more likely a combination of mild pH drop, surface effect and a protective coating than deep tenderisation; the only controlled data (acid whey) showed no tenderness effect in chicken. Therefore expect Tikka's yoghurt to add flavour and some surface softness, not to fix toughness.
- Because acid pulls pH toward the water-holding minimum, a long vinegar/mustard marinade is plausibly neutral-to-slightly-negative for juiciness in breast, but the 48 h ACV study found better shear force, so toughness is not the risk; juiciness/mushy surface is. No quantified cook-loss data found for chicken breast.
- Freezing in the marinade followed by thaw adds an independent drip/softening effect: supercooled/fast-frozen chicken breast shows increased drip loss; one study reported frozen breasts less tender, another that freeze-thaw lowered shear force. Evidence conflicts; not specific to marinated meat. [PR via search summaries] — [Thai freeze-thaw record](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/APST/article/view/82997); [SciELO thawing methods](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbca/a/fC7R45ZBLQfVfzWHSkGKQCs/abstract/?lang=en)

### Gaps
- No chicken-breast study of real (Greek-style) yoghurt marinades with cook loss and shear force; calcium/enzyme mechanisms not evidenced.
- No time-course study (2, 6, 12, 24+ h) of vinegar/mustard marinades on broiler breast cook loss.
- No study of marinating then freezing then thawing chicken breast in the marinade.

## Commercial practice (phosphate + salt injection) and whether a home equivalent makes sense

### Takeaway
Processors use salt plus polyphosphates (STPP) by injection/tumbling, typically in studies at 0.35% STP plus 0.5-1.0% NaCl, to raise yield and water-holding capacity; bicarbonate has been tested as a phosphate replacement because of phosphate's nutritional drawbacks. At home, STPP gave no extra benefit over salt in the one simple-soak test I found, so it is not sensible.

### Cited Findings
- Phosphates raise yield and water-holding capacity but "some nutritional drawbacks have been recently evidenced," motivating bicarbonate as an alternative; in 2010 work bicarbonate matched phosphates' marination performance. [PR, Petracci group] — [Petracci 2010 record](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/91139) (via search summary)
- Tumbled broiler breast: 0.35% STP with 0.5-1.0% NaCl effective (Mississippi State); 0.75% NaCl + 0.45% STPP best shear; 1.25% NaCl + 0.35% STPP best cook yield (Pakistan study). [PR] — [Scholars Junction](https://scholarsjunction.msstate.edu/td/1685); [JAPS](https://thejaps.org.pk/manuscript/vs-18-0067.pdf)
- Bicarbonate 0.3% beat 0.3% tripolyphosphate on cook loss (30.7 vs 33.4%) and water retention. [PR] — [Najah record](https://staff.najah.edu/en/publications/803/)
- Home test: STPP at 0.1% (of total weight) in a salt brine or alone made no difference to cooked yield; author advises against adding STPP to a brine. [AN, n=1] — [Modernist Pantry](https://blog.modernistpantry.com/advice/can-sodium-tripolyphosphate-improve-brining/)
- Synergy between bicarbonate and phosphate reported in a review. [PR review via search summary] — [Petracci 2014 listing](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpsa/51/2/51_0130079/_article)

### Inferences
- A "home phosphate" gives no demonstrated advantage over simply salting the meat, adds an additive (his notes say no new supplements; this is food-additive rather than supplement but gains nothing here), and STPP is mainly useful in tumbled/emulsified products. Not recommended.
- The home equivalent of the commercial result is salt (about 0.75-1.0%) plus optionally a modest bicarbonate dose.

### Gaps
- No home-scale phosphate dosing guidance from a reputable source; phosphate sources for home (e.g., "Kansui", baking powder) not researched.

## Sodium implications of each option; retention after rinsing

### Takeaway
Sodium from bicarbonate depends almost entirely on how much is rinsed off: a cooking-source dose of 3/4 tsp per 250 g is 945 mg sodium per 250 g if none were rinsed away [CALC], versus about 120 mg per 150 g serving at the studied 0.3%. I found no data on how much sodium remains after rinsing. Salt is the bigger lever: a 1% dry brine is about 3.9 g sodium per kg meat before losses.

### Cited Findings
- A level teaspoon of baking soda (4.6 g) contains about 1,230-1,260 mg sodium (USDA-derived listing 1,258.6 mg; Arm & Hammer 616 mg per half tsp). [nutrition listing / label] — [Soupersage](https://www.soupersage.com/nutrition-calories-protein/baking-soda); [search summary of label](https://www.thedonutwhole.com/is-baking-soda-full-of-sodium/)
- Brined chicken breast: 6 oz cooked boneless, skinless breast brined 1 h in 2 quarts water with 1/4 cup salt added about 270 mg sodium (lab-analysed, naturally occurring sodium subtracted). [ET] — [ATK/CI](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/cooksillustrated/how_tos/8474-how-much-sodium-is-in-brined-food)
- A dietitian rule of thumb: about 20-25% of the sodium in a meat/poultry brine is taken up; data largely proprietary. [AN] — [EatRightPro forum](https://community.eatrightpro.org/discussion/recipe-analysis-question-sodium-from-brining)
- Raw/cooked chicken breast baseline sodium quoted at about 353 mg per 100 g in one local news item (brine unknown); not usable as unseasoned baseline. [AN, unreliable] — [Post Bulletin](https://www.postbulletin.com/lifestyle/ask-the-cook-salt-content-of-brined-meats)
- Sodium content of compounds [CALC from molar masses]: NaHCO3 (84.0 g/mol) 27.4% Na (matches the user's figure); NaCl 39.3% Na.
- Sodium arithmetic for his budget [CALC, assuming 150 g cooked serving from about 200 g raw; cooking loss ignored; all values are upper bounds assuming everything stays in the meat]:
  - Bicarbonate at 0.3% of meat weight = 3 g/kg = 822 mg Na/kg = about 165 mg per 200 g raw piece.
  - Bicarbonate at cooking-source dose 1.35-1.4% (before rinsing) = about 13.5-14 g/kg = about 3,700-3,800 mg Na/kg = about 740-760 mg per 200 g raw if nothing rinsed off.
  - Dry brine 0.75% salt = 7.5 g/kg = 2,950 mg Na/kg = about 590 mg per 200 g raw; 1.0% = 3,930 mg/kg = about 790 mg per 200 g.
  - His marinades as stated: Mustard 1,500 mg/kg (about 300 mg per 200 g); Tikka 3,000 mg/kg (about 600 mg per 200 g); his own figures are marinade totals and not necessarily absorbed.
  - Per-day budget context: baseline about 1,300 mg/day and target at most 2,000 mg/day leaves about 700 mg of headroom.

### Inferences
- Sodium absorbed from a marinade is only a fraction of the sodium supplied (20-25% rule of thumb for brines; the ATK test: 270 mg in a 6 oz cooked breast from a 1 h brine), so his marinade totals (1,500 and 3,000 mg/kg) probably overstate what ends up in the meat, particularly since he discards the marinade after thaw. This is inference; the rule of thumb is not rigorous.
- Bicarbonate at the studied level adds a modest sodium load compared with salt, and rinsing removes an unknown amount. A dry brine at 0.75-1.0% plus current marinade would roughly add 590-790 mg per 200 g raw piece (upper bound), a large fraction of his headroom, so replacing marinade salt rather than adding to it is the sodium-neutral approach. This is the report writer's decision to frame; I give only the arithmetic.
- Hepatitis B context: baking soda, salt and these seasonings are ordinary food ingredients; I did not research liver-specific issues of bicarbonate (not requested and not assessed).

### Gaps
- No measured sodium retention in chicken after a baking-soda soak and rinse.
- No measured sodium uptake for dry-brined chicken breast; the 20-25% figure is a forum-level rule of thumb.
- The full Inguglia et al. 2019 (Foods) paper with mg/100 g sodium uptake for chicken breast brined 1, 3 and 6 h was not accessed (abstract-level only: ultrasound raised uptake vs control) — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/71605d5bac3a45b885f180141d733ca7).

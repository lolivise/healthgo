# Air-fryer chicken breast: juicy cooking, safe endpoint, storage and reheating (Perth, Australia home cook)

Evidence grades used on every claim: **[PR]** peer-reviewed paper; **[OFF]** official guidance (FSANZ, state health, USDA); **[EXP]** expert-tested (ThermoWorks, Serious Eats, Baldwin, calculated tables); **[ANEC]** recipe sites, forum, single home cook. Unless noted, I read the page or PDF text myself. Items marked "search-summary only" come from a search snippet and were not verified in full text.

## 1. Why breast goes dry and tough: end-temperature vs moisture loss and shear force

### Takeaway
Cooking loss in breast rises steadily with end-point temperature, and in the data found it is steepest above about 70-75 C. Shear force (toughness) is flat across about 75-89 C in one study and only jumps at 94 C, so "dry" (water loss) shows up well before "tough". The mechanism is protein denaturation and fibre shrinkage that squeezes water out; collagen is not the main driver in breast. No single study found tests 60/65/70/75/80 C side by side, so the table below stitches together separate studies that used different methods.

### Cited Findings
- **Broiler breast, grilling oven, held 5 min at room temp after removal. Cooking loss / shear force / water-holding capacity / sarcomere length by end-point temperature.** [PR]
  - 75 C: 24.75 % / 3.87 kg / 54.25 % / 1.02 um
  - 83 C: 31.5 % / 4.04 kg / 46.88 % / 0.86 um
  - 89 C: 34.08 % / 4.16 kg / 41.88 % / 0.78 um
  - 94 C: 36.63 % / 6.03 kg / 39.87 % / 0.71 um
  - Cooking loss rose significantly at every step (p<0.01). Shear force did not differ significantly between 75, 83 and 89 C, and rose significantly only at 94 C. Sarcomere length fell steadily with temperature (muscle fibre shrinkage). Muscle fibre diameter did not differ significantly. SDS-PAGE showed myofibrillar protein bands weakening at higher end-points. Sample pieces were small (about 10-12 g each, from ~200 g of breast). — [Muthulakshmi et al. 2022, Int J Bio-resource & Stress Mgmt 13(3):205-212](https://ojs.pphouse.org/index.php/IJBSM/article/download/4201/1667/3337)
- **Sous-vide vs convection oven, chicken breast (Park et al. 2020, Poultry Science 99(6):3286-3291).** [PR]
  - Control, convection oven to 71 C internal: cooking loss 26.5 %, Warner-Bratzler shear 29.1 N, lowest juiciness sensory score (2.86).
  - 60 C: 1 h 6.58 %, 2 h 11.7 %, 3 h 13.2 % cooking loss; shear 21.7 / 23.3 / 26.0 N.
  - 70 C: 1 h 17.3 %, 2 h 22.4 %, 3 h 25.3 % cooking loss; shear 25.3 / 25.8 / 28.5 N.
  - 60 C for 1 h had the highest juiciness score (6.62). The abstract-level fetch did not give the sensory scale. Cooking loss was used as the water-holding indicator (moisture not measured directly). — [Park et al. 2020 via PMC7597729](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7597729)
- Same pattern in the literature summary: a core-temperature series reports cooking loss increasing significantly from 70 to 90 C, with 80 C a critical point for shear force; another reports significant cooking-loss increase over 65-85 C and rupture of endomysium/perimysium at 75 C and above. **Search-summary only; I could not open the paper (403).** [PR, unverified detail] — [SCUT journal record (2012, Chinese)](https://zrb.bjb.scut.edu.cn/CN/Y2012/V40/I8/116)
- The 2022 Indian paper's introduction (citing others) states myosin starts to denature about 40-60 C and actin about 80 C, and that sarcomere shrinkage from myofibrillar denaturation can increase toughness; it also cites Chumngoen et al. (2016) as finding cooking losses "considerable at temperatures exceeding 80 C". These are secondhand citations. [PR, secondhand] — [Muthulakshmi et al. 2022](https://ojs.pphouse.org/index.php/IJBSM/article/download/4201/1667/3337)
- Tornberg 2005 (Meat Science 70:493-508) abstract: fibrous (myofibrillar) proteins contract on heating; myofibrillar gelation begins 45-50 C; most sarcoplasmic proteins aggregate at 40-60 C; collagen denatures at roughly 53-63 C and then shrinks. Abstract only. [PR] — [Lund University record](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/152058)
- Heating rate matters: in Gal et al. 2022 (Poultry Science 101(7)), lowest cooking loss and shear force came from methods with higher heating rates/temperatures and shorter cooking times; grilled chicken breast fillets scored high sensory. Abstract-level only; no numbers retrieved. [PR] — [Gal et al. 2022, PMC9189220](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9189220/)
- Chicken breast texture by temperature (secondhand summary of Kenji Lopez-Alt in a ThermoWorks guide): about 150 F (65.6 C) white, opaque, juicy, firm; stringy near 155 F (68 C); dry by 160 F (71 C); his favourite doneness 145 F (63 C). I could not fetch the original Serious Eats piece, and the ThermoWorks page I fetched did not contain this chart, so treat as unverified secondhand. [EXP, secondhand] — [ThermoWorks blog (search summary)](https://blog.thermoworks.com/3-keys-to-perfect-chicken-breasts/)
- ThermoWorks article does say cooking to 165 F "actually overcooks it" and that a lower target gives "more tender and juicy" chicken. [EXP] — [ThermoWorks](https://blog.thermoworks.com/3-keys-to-perfect-chicken-breasts/)
- Freeze-thaw (relevant to this cook's pipeline): a 2014 broiler-breast study found freeze-thaw produced greater drip loss and lower shear force; longer chilled storage raised drip and cooking losses; repeated freeze-thaw cycles (4 cycles) lowered water-holding capacity; cold-water thawing gave lower drip loss and better tenderness than other thaw methods in a separate study. Search-summary only; none tested marinated meat. [PR, unverified detail] — [Meat quality of chicken breast, different thawing methods (SciELO)](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbca/a/fC7R45ZBLQfVfzWHSkGKQCs/abstract/?lang=en); [Thai APST article](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/APST/article/view/82997)

### Inferences
- Across these studies, a roughly 70-75 C end-point loses about 25 % of raw weight and 89-94 C loses about 34-37 %. Cooking to 60 C (sous vide) lost about 7-13 %. Moving from about 75 C to about 65 C should recover a meaningful share of that, but the air-fryer-specific figure is not measured here.
- "Chewy/tough" at a normal 74 C is more likely a combination of dry fibres and overshoot on thin edges than of collagen. The only strong shear-force jump in the retrieved data is at 94 C, so toughness from a plain overshoot of a few degrees is plausible but not shown quantitatively in breast; much of the perceived toughness is probably dryness. (Inference, not directly sourced.)
- Small chunks in a hot air fryer: edges and thin parts can reach 85-95 C while the centre is at target, so spread of temperature inside the piece matters more than the nominal pull temperature (see section 4, ThermoWorks gradient data).
- Freeze-thaw adds drip loss before cooking begins; discarding marinade after thaw and patting dry removes surface water (see section 5).

### Gaps
- No single paper found with cooking loss and shear force at exactly 60/65/70/75/80 C for breast in dry-heat (oven/air-fryer) cooking.
- No peer-reviewed air-fryer-specific breast study retrieved.
- Original Serious Eats and America's Test Kitchen texture-vs-temperature figures could not be accessed (fetch blocked).
- Effect of marinades (mayo/honey/mustard, Greek yoghurt) on cooking loss after freezing not found.

## 2. Food-safety endpoint: Australian guidance vs USDA vs time-temperature lethality

### Takeaway
Australian guidance (FSANZ and Food Safety Information Council) says 75 C at the centre for chicken; the US says 165 F (73.9 C). The USDA FSIS lethality table for chicken shows a 7-log Salmonella kill is reached by holding about 65.6 C (150 F) for 2.7 min, 68.3 C (155 F) for 45 s, 70 C (158 F) for 22 s, so pulling at about 66-70 C and letting the piece sit hot is consistent with the science, but only if the coldest point reaches the temperature, which cannot be confirmed without a thermometer. The 75 C instant rule is a simple, conservative single number, not the only route to safe.

### Cited Findings
- **Australia, FSIC:** cook poultry until a meat thermometer shows 75 C "in the thickest part of the thigh"; "cooking poultry to 75C in the centre of a fillet or the thickest part of the thigh will kill any bacteria." Recommends probe thermometers as "readily available, easy to use". [OFF-adjacent, non-government peak body] — [Food Safety Information Council](https://www.foodsafety.asn.au/?p=8345)
- **Australia, FSANZ:** "cook chicken and mince to an internal temperature of >=75 C" is given as an example of a processing step known to achieve food safety. It is presented as a recommendation, not a legal requirement, with no time-temperature alternative stated; the page also says to "ensure the time and temperature makes food safe". [OFF] — [FSANZ Processing food safely](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/food-safety/fact-sheets/processing-food-safely)
- FSANZ consumer-side summary (search-summary): Salmonella and Campylobacter are killed by cooking; cook chicken until no pink is visible and juices run clear. [OFF] — [FSANZ listeria/consumer advice PDF](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/sites/default/files/consumer/safety/listeria/Documents/listeria-1.pdf) (search result, not fully read; the "no pink" wording reached me via search-summary only)
- **USA:** poultry 165 F (73.9 C) by food thermometer; USDA says cook to minimum internal temps "before removing from the heat source". Search-summary only (fsis.usda.gov and foodsafety.gov pages returned 403 to my fetches). [OFF] — [FSIS Safe Minimum Internal Temperature chart](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-temperature-chart)
- **USDA FSIS time-temperature table, chicken, 2 % fat, time for 7-log10 Salmonella lethality** (the FSIS RTE poultry table, read from a Riverstone Health copy of FSIS "RTE_Poultry_Tables"; this is the table for the *minimum temperature reached in all parts of the product*, dwell time as listed). [OFF] — [Riverstone Health PDF copy of FSIS tables](https://riverstonehealth.org/wp-content/uploads/SousVide_LethalityChart_Poultry.pdf)

[OFF] — [FSIS / MSU summary (search-summary)](https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/time-temperature-tables-for-cooking-ready-to-eat-poultry-products)
- **Baldwin pasteurisation table for poultry (sous vide, 7-log Salmonella and 6-log Listeria, start 5 C):** thickness matters because the centre must heat. E.g. 20 mm piece needs 40 min at 65 C bath, 45 min at 64 C, 50 min at 63 C, 1 h 15 at 60 C; 15 mm piece 30 min at 65 C. The page states it is for poultry generally, not breast specifically. - **Serious Eats / ThermoWorks (secondhand):** 165 F kills Salmonella "almost instantly"; 155 F (68 C) works if held for 54.4 seconds (an alternative table figure, slightly different from FSIS 45 s). ThermoWorks says pull at 155 F and tent; a 5-7 F (3-4 C) carryover rise "can be expected if the meat is kept from cooling too quickly". [EXP] — [ThermoWorks](https://blog.thermoworks.com/3-keys-to-perfect-chicken-breasts/)
- **Chronic liver disease and food safety, general guidance (not poultry-specific):** a Delaware state brochure lists liver disease among conditions that raise risk from raw/undercooked animal foods; Children's Hospital of Philadelphia liver-transplant guidance advises people with chronic liver disease to avoid raw animal foods (especially oysters: Vibrio vulnificus is "fatal in up to 50 %" of people with liver disorders) and eat foods hot and well cooked. Both from search snippets, not read in full. [OFF-adjacent] — [Delaware DPH brochure](https://dhss.delaware.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/10/dph/pdf/ofpconadbro.pdf); [CHOP liver food safety](https://chp.edu/our-services/transplant/liver/patient-family-resources/liver-transplant-safety/food-safety)
- **Salmonella in cirrhosis (search-summary):** a case series of 23 patients with cirrhosis and non-typhoidal Salmonella bacteraemia reported hospital mortality of 48 % and that most cases were community-acquired; authors say such cases are sporadic in the literature. Cirrhosis in general raises bacteraemia risk (Danish standardised incidence ratio 10.5). These describe outcomes after bacteraemia, not the baseline risk of getting it from food. [PR, unverified detail] — [TMU: non-typhoidal Salmonella bacteremia in cirrhosis](https://hub.tmu.edu.tw/en/publications/nontyphoid-salmonella-bacteremia-in-patients-with-liver-cirrhosis/); [Danish bacteremia in cirrhosis via PMC11344699](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11344699/)
- Campylobacter in poultry livers needs 70-80 C for at least 2 min (New Zealand survey, search-summary). This is for liver, not breast. [PR, secondhand] — [barfblog summary](https://barfblog.com/page/139)

### Inferences
- **Honest risk framing for pulling at about 65-68 C:** the FSIS table says that if every part of a lean piece reaches 65.6 C and stays there about 3 minutes (or 68.3 C for about 45 s), the Salmonella design lethality is met. That is how US restaurants and sous-vide cooks justify below-165 F doneness. It does not apply if the cold spot is lower than the reading, if the probe is misplaced, or if the piece is thin enough that it cools quickly after leaving the air fryer. Both Australia's 75 C and USDA's 165 F are an instant-kill rule that builds in margin for those errors. The table is for Salmonella only; I found no equivalent source for Campylobacter or Listeria on the pulled-temperature question.
- **Without a thermometer** the comparison is not "75 C vs 68 C"; it is "guessing at doneness" vs a reading. Visual doneness checks are unreliable, so he gets no verified safety margin at any temperature. The thermometer is therefore both the safety fix and the juiciness fix.
- **Hepatitis B and poultry:** I found no source that states controlled chronic hepatitis B (without cirrhosis) raises Salmonella/Campylobacter risk from chicken, and none that says it does not. Evidence is about cirrhosis (more severe bacteraemia outcomes) and general advice for "chronic liver disease" to eat meat well cooked, with Vibrio (raw seafood) the liver-specific high-risk case. A reasonable inference is that for him the 75 C / 165 F instant margin is the conservative choice and the time-temperature route (65.6 C for 3 min etc.) is only appropriate if a verified thermometer reading is taken in the coldest piece; it is a question for his doctor/dietitian, not something the research settles.
- The marinade is discarded after thaw and the chicken is cooked in small pieces, which removes the main raw-juice cross-contamination route for the cooked product; hygiene of raw handling remains a separate point. (Inference, no source.)

### Gaps
- No source found on whether carryover heat alone is relied on in Australian guidance.
- No Australian official statement on time-temperature alternatives for home cooks (FSANZ page gives 75 C only for businesses/"example" use).
- No source on risk of chronic hepatitis B without cirrhosis and foodborne bacterial infection.
- FSIS and foodsafety.gov pages returned 403, so USDA 165 F text is from search summaries.

## 3. Carryover cooking and resting for small pieces

### Takeaway
Reported carryover for a whole breast is about 3-4 C after removal (ThermoWorks, with the meat "kept from cooling too quickly"); general guides give 3-5 C for 5-10 min. No source found that measures carryover in small 2-3 cm pieces or strips; small pieces have less thermal mass, so any rise is probably small and brief.

### Cited Findings
- ThermoWorks: pull at 155 F (68 C), tent, expect "a 5-7 F (3-4 C)" rise if kept from cooling too quickly. [EXP] — [ThermoWorks](https://blog.thermoworks.com/3-keys-to-perfect-chicken-breasts/)
- General guides: chicken breast carryover roughly 4-6 C or 5-8 F, rest 5-10 min; one forum commenter says rise for small cuts is "a few degrees at most". [ANEC] — [Thermapen UK](https://thermapen.co.uk/blogs/inspiration/why-meat-keeps-cooking-as-it-rests); [BBQ Host](https://bbqhost.com/understanding-carryover-cooking/); [Egghead forum](https://eggheadforum.com/discussion/comment/2459320)
- Recipe sources say resting 5 min (tightly covered) lets residual heat finish and juices settle for air-fried breast. [ANEC] — [thekitchengirl.com](https://thekitchengirl.com/air-fryer-chicken-breast/) (via search summary)
- ThermoWorks: unflattened breast can have a centre-to-edge temperature swing up to 53 F (about 29 C); pounding to even thickness reduces it to 19 F (about 11 C). "Meat is only as cooked as its lowest temperature." [EXP] — [ThermoWorks](https://blog.thermoworks.com/3-keys-to-perfect-chicken-breasts/)
- Park 2020's oven control (71 C internal) had the worst juiciness; the Indian grilling study held samples at room temperature for 5 min after reaching target, and cooking loss still scaled with target. [PR] — see section 1.

### Inferences
- For small pieces the useful part of resting is holding them hot in a covered bowl to complete the dwell time (see FSIS table) rather than to redistribute juices; the benefit for juiciness in small pieces is not quantified in anything found. If aiming at about 68 C, expect the finished temperature after a 2-3 min covered rest to land perhaps 1-3 C higher (guess from the above, not measured).
- Do not slice small pieces open to check; each cut releases juice. Check by probe.

### Gaps
- No measured carryover data for 2-3 cm chunks cooked in an air fryer.
- No controlled test found on resting vs not resting for small pieces.

## 4. Air-fryer technique, timing, thermometer use

### Takeaway
There is no peer-reviewed air-fryer breast protocol found. The practical consensus (all recipe-level, [ANEC]) is: uniform pieces, no crowding, moderate-to-hot air (about 180-200 C), check core temperature rather than time, pull a little under target and rest. Times for 2-3 cm pieces are roughly 8-15 min at 200 C for crumbed strips; plain chunks should be shorter. Treat all times as starting points to calibrate with a thermometer.

### Cited Findings
- Recipe norms for whole breasts: 380 F (about 193 C) 8 min, flip, 4-6 min more; pull 155-160 F (68-71 C) and rest because heat keeps rising; add 2-3 min per side from frozen; rest 5-10 min; do not overcrowd; light oil helps browning and seasoning cling. [ANEC] — [Six Sisters / Kitchen Girl / others via search summary](https://thekitchengirl.com/air-fryer-chicken-breast/)
- Breaded strips at 200 C: 10-12 min flipping halfway (Cuisinart UK); 10 min then 3-5 more min after turning (Simply Delicious); Panasonic UK: 7 min then 3-5 min after flipping to reach 73 C; fajita strips 200 C 10 min then check at least 72 C; cut to similar sizes so small pieces do not dry out before thick ones finish. [ANEC] — [Cuisinart UK](https://www.cuisinart.co.uk/crispy-chicken-tenders-recipe.html); [Panasonic UK](https://experience-fresh.panasonic.co.uk/?p=26773); [K's Cuisine](https://kscuisine.com/wprm_print/air-fryer-chicken-strips) (via search summary)
- Brining suggestion in one recipe: 8 cups water with 1/2 cup table salt, 30-60 min. [ANEC] — recipe site via search summary.
- Even thickness: pounding to uniform thickness cuts the centre-to-edge gradient from 53 F to 19 F (ThermoWorks, above). [EXP]
- Which cooking method gave lowest cooking loss in Gal et al. 2022: higher heating rate and shorter time. [PR] (see section 1).
- Use a thermometer at the lowest point: "Meat is only as cooked as its lowest temperature" (ThermoWorks). [EXP]
- Thermometers: CHOICE lists the Weber Instant Read Thermometer (about $27, 0-230 C) but ratings are members-only; independent reviewers rate ThermoWorks Thermapen ONE as most accurate/fast, Lavatools Javelin PRO Duo as best value, ThermoPop 2 as affordable (one reviewer disagreed), and a TempPro-type unit at about $26 with 3-4 s read. I found no Australian price list or Australian-tested comparison. [ANEC] — [CHOICE Weber listing](https://www.choice.com.au/products/home-and-living/kitchen/utensils/weber-instant-read-thermometer-6750); [Prudent Reviews](https://prudentreviews.com/best-instant-read-thermometer/); [Smoked BBQ Source ThermoPop 2](https://www.smokedbbqsource.com/?p=2279); [NestPath AU pick list](https://nestpath.com.au/compare/best-meat-thermometer-australia)
- Probe placement for thin foods (side insertion) did not come back from any official source I could access.
- Sugary marinades: sources agree that honey/sugar burn before chicken is done. Cook chicken to temperature first, then brush on glaze for only the last 2-5 min, optionally at about 195-200 C for 2 min to set it. "Honey burns extremely quickly." [ANEC] — [Girl Carnivore](https://girlcarnivore.com/air-fryer-bbq-chicken/); [Little Bit Recipes](https://littlebitrecipes.com/wprm_print/air-fryer-boneless-chicken-thighs-with-honey-mustard); [Cooked by Julie](https://www.cookedbyjulie.com/wprm_print/air-fryer-honey-bbq-wings)

### Inferences
- Because his marinade is discarded after thaw, the burn risk comes from residual honey/mayo film on the surface; patting dry and leaving a thin film, or re-applying sugar glaze only at the end (all anecdote-based), should help. No test of a thawed, discarded-marinade piece was found.
- Cutting across the grain, butterflied vs chunk, preheat and flipping: no source found that tests these for juiciness; the only support is uniform thickness (ThermoWorks gradient) and "similar sizes" (recipe advice). I did not find a source supporting 180 vs 200 C for juiciness specifically beyond the Gal et al. heating-rate result.
- Small pieces will reach 68-70 C quickly; a probe reading after about 6-8 min at 200 C and each minute thereafter is a sensible calibration plan, to be refined by his own first few batches (inference, not sourced).

### Gaps
- No lab-tested air-fryer breast time/temperature data; no ATK or Serious Eats air-fryer breast testing retrieved.
- No source for grain direction, butterfly vs chunk, preheating or flipping effects on juiciness.
- No Australian-market thermometer prices or Australian lab tests beyond a members-only CHOICE page.
- Correct thermometer placement for thin cuts not verified from an official source.

## 5. Storage and reheating of cooked breast; cook daily or batch

### Takeaway
Australian guidance: refrigerate within 2 hours and eat within 3 days (NSW), fridge at or below 5 C (FSIC), and it differs on reheating temperature: FSIC says reheat leftovers to 75 C in the centre; NSW Food Authority and FSANZ say 60 C or hotter ("steaming hot"). USDA says 3-4 days and 165 F. The best-evidenced way to limit moisture loss on reheating is gentler heat (steam/hot water; microwave lost the most water in a braised-chicken study); every reheat also adds more cooking, so cooking a batch slightly under, or daily-cooking the portion, protects texture.

### Cited Findings
- **NSW Food Authority leftovers advice (search-summary; direct fetch timed out):** cool, cover and refrigerate or freeze within 2 hours; eat within 3 days (rice/pasta within 2); reheat "steaming hot", minimum 60 C; stir microwaved food. [OFF] — [NSW Food Authority](https://www.foodauthority.nsw.gov.au/node/23948)
- **FSIC:** leftovers "need to be reheated to 75 C in the centre", check with a meat thermometer or microwave auto-reheat; discard higher-risk foods (meat, poultry, eggs) if not eaten that day in the lunch-box context; fridge 5 C or below; items left out under 2 h can be refrigerated, over 4 h thrown away. No fridge-days figure given on the pages I read. [OFF-adjacent] — [FSIC leftovers](https://www.foodsafety.asn.au/love-those-leftovers-back-to-school-and-work-lunch-box-food-safety-advice-16-january-2018/); [FSIC](https://www.foodsafety.asn.au/?p=8345)
- **FSANZ:** "Reheat foods to >=60 C as quickly as possible" (ideally within 2 h); avoid reheating potentially hazardous food more than once; no mention of 75 C on the cooling/reheating page (Standard 3.2.2 clause 7 context). [OFF] — [FSANZ Cooling and reheating food](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/food-safety/fact-sheets/cooling-and-reheating-food); [FSANZ Processing food safely](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/food-safety/fact-sheets/processing-food-safely)
- Tasmania health: reheat until steaming hot (at least 60 C); meat/chicken in fridge 1-2 days raw, otherwise freeze. [OFF] — [Tasmania Dept of Health](https://health.tas.gov.au/node/7936) (search-summary)
- Healthdirect Australia: uncooked poultry 3 days in fridge below 5 C. [OFF] — [healthdirect infographic](https://healthdirect.gov.au/food-safety-infographic) (search-summary)
- **USA:** cooked leftovers 3-4 days in fridge, reheat to 165 F by thermometer; microwave: stir, cover, rotate; covering retains moisture; check cold spots. [OFF] — [FSIS Leftovers and Food Safety](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/leftovers-and-food-safety) (search-summary; 403 on fetch)
- **Reheating method study (Liu et al. 2025, Foods 14:868, pre-cooked braised chicken, commercial vacuum pack, heated to 72.5 C centre).** Microwave (800 W, 2 min), boiling water in bag (8 min), steam (14 min). Microwave had significantly greater reheating loss and moisture loss than boiling-water and steam; steam had the lowest reheating loss and least texture change. Hardness (g): control 478, microwave 1096, boiling water 958, steam 715; chewiness (g-mm): control 544, MW 1758, WB 1469, ST 956. Not breast and a commercial braised product, but a direct cooked-chicken reheat comparison. [PR] — [Liu et al. 2025](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/foods/foods-14-00868/article_deploy/foods-14-00868.pdf)
- Another cooked-chicken-breast reheating study (frozen prepared chicken breast, 2024, Chinese journal; search-summary): water content did not differ significantly among reheating methods; long steam reheating caused marked protein oxidation and flavour deterioration; microwave and infrared-microwave were fast and well balanced; air frying judged most appropriate. This conflicts with the braised-chicken study on microwave moisture. [PR, unverified detail] — [Food Science and Technology, 10.13386/j.issn1002-0306.2024100083](https://www.spgykj.com/en/article/doi/10.13386/j.issn1002-0306.2024100083)
- 2025 precooked chicken breast study (search-summary): thawing at 4 C then steam reheating gave quality closest to fresh cooked; this combination limited loss of immobilised water versus room-temperature thaw plus microwave. [PR, unverified detail] — [Food Science and Technology 2025, 10.13386/j.issn1002-0306.2025080073](https://www.spgykj.com/en/article/doi/10.13386/j.issn1002-0306.2025080073)
- 1993 Virginia Tech thesis: heating temperature, regardless of method, significantly affected oxidative deterioration (warmed-over flavour) in precooked, refrigerated chicken breast. Abstract only. [PR] — [VT thesis](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/items/1c86f670-1b4b-4029-b185-bb6fb9f979a0)
- Higher-temperature cooking raised lipid oxidation (TBARS 0.27 at 75 C to 0.40 at 94 C), which is the chemistry behind warmed-over off-flavour. [PR] — [Muthulakshmi et al. 2022](https://ojs.pphouse.org/index.php/IJBSM/article/download/4201/1667/3337)
- Web claims about exact microwave-vs-other moisture percentages (from "Cornell Food Physics Lab", etc.) could not be traced to a real study; I excluded them.

### Inferences
- **Batch vs daily:** a 350 g portion cooked and eaten the same day avoids the extra moisture loss from reheating and the oxidation of stored cooked meat. If batch cooking, a bulk batch cooked to about 65-68 C then reheated to a safe temperature adds a second heating step. Since the FSIS table gives lethality for the total dwell, cooking a bit under then reheating is not credited in any source found; the safe course is to ensure the final reheat is 75 C (FSIC) or at least steaming hot 60 C+ (NSW/FSANZ) and not rely on the first cook for safety. (Inference.)
- **Which Australian reheat figure to follow:** FSIC's 75 C is the stricter number and is what a risk-averse person with a chronic liver condition would adopt; NSW/FSANZ's 60 C is the legal-style business figure. A cooked breast already cooked to a verified safe temperature and stored cold is a different risk case from raw; this note does not settle which applies to him.
- **Gentle reheating (practical, not directly tested):** covered microwave on medium power with a splash of water, or warm water/steam, follows from the braised-chicken finding that intensity drives reheat loss; slicing after reheating and reheating in portions are plausible but not sourced.
- Cooked chicken frozen as portions is an alternative to the 3-day fridge limit for a 4.5 kg per fortnight volume: FSANZ/Tasmania advise freezing what will not be eaten within the fridge window. Evidence on cooked-breast freezer life and quality was not found.

### Gaps
- No Australian official fridge-days figure read directly for cooked chicken on a primary government page (NSW fetch timed out; "within 3 days" is a search summary). FSIC pages gave no day figure.
- No controlled test of "cook slightly under then reheat" for breast.
- No source on sous-vide-style warm-water reheat of breast, nor medium-power microwave settings.
- Cooked-breast freezer storage life not found.

## 6. Forgiving alternatives (brief comparison)

### Takeaway
Gentle methods lose much less water: sous vide at 60 C for 1 h had 6.6 % cooking loss against 26.5 % for a convection oven to 71 C in one paper, and was judged juiciest. Poaching/steaming were not directly tested for breast in anything retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Sous vide at 60-70 C: cooking loss 6.6-25.3 % depending on time; oven control to 71 C: 26.5 %. [PR] — [Park et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7597729)
- Sous-vide chicken breast: 60 C 2 h and 80 C 0.5 h gave the lowest shear force in a separate study (search summary). [PR, unverified detail] — [Agris record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df9e760f3e94b9e5daa73e)
- Safety for sous vide depends on time, thickness and temperature: Baldwin gives 15 mm at 65 C about 30 min, 25 mm about 55 min (7-log Salmonella, 6-log Listeria). [EXP] — [Baldwin](https://www.douglasbaldwin.com/sous-vide.html)
- Serious Eats via Anova: brining unnecessary for sous vide chicken. [EXP, secondhand] — [AOL repost of Food Lab guide (search summary)](https://www.aol.com/lifestyle/food-labs-complete-guide-sous-074500281.html)
- Steam was the gentlest reheating method for cooked chicken in Liu et al. 2025 (section 5). [PR]

### Inferences
- A sous-vide cooker or simple poach-then-pan/air-fry sear is the most forgiving route for a no-thermometer cook because the water temperature, not timing by eye, sets the endpoint; steaming/poaching of thin strips is likely similar but not evidenced here.

### Gaps
- No source retrieved comparing poaching or steaming raw breast directly with air frying.
- Original Food Lab pages not accessible.

# Rapid weight loss, facial aging ("Ozempic face"), collagen and evidence-based skin-aging prevention

Scope note: researched 2026-10-25 for a 36-year-old Taiwanese man in Perth (high UV), diet-and-training cut 102 → ~88 kg since 2026-07-15 (~1 %/week, slowing), target 80–83 kg, not on GLP-1 drugs, hepatitis B (no new supplements). Every claim below has a link. "Verified" means the primary paper or its abstract/full text was read; "secondary only" means the figure was seen only in a news, trade or review summary and the primary was not opened.

## (a) Does rapid weight loss age the face, and why? ("Ozempic face" literature 2023–2026, facial anatomy, perceived-age studies)

### Takeaway
The peer-reviewed "Ozempic face" literature is almost entirely reviews, opinion pieces, case reports and surveys. The mechanism the reviews agree on is loss of facial fat volume, plus skin that does not retract, after *large* weight loss. There is **no controlled imaging study** comparing GLP-1 loss with diet or bariatric loss, and the only facial-volume number for GLP-1 users comes from a 5-person conference abstract. Weight loss does make faces look older in bariatric (massive-loss) cohorts, and the effect is clearly larger with more weight lost and at older ages.

### Cited Findings
**Systematic reviews (best available evidence)**
- Daneshgaran, Shauly & Gould, *Aesthetic Surgery Journal Open Forum* 2025 (systematic review, 23 articles, PubMed to Feb 2025). Included 5 correspondence pieces, 5 reviews, 4 case reports, 3 retrospective cohorts, 2 viewpoints, 2 special-focus pieces, 1 survey and 1 systematic review. **Only 3 studies had a control group**, and no prospective or patient-reported-outcome studies existed. No facial imaging or volume data were reported. Verbatim: *"Evidence to suggest that GLP-1 receptor agonists preferentially result in facial fat atrophy is lacking."* The authors suggest "Ozempic face" may be a transient media trend rather than a distinct drug side effect. Verified. — [Daneshgaran et al. 2025, ASJ Open Forum (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232544/)
- Jafar, Jacob, Kao & Ho, *ASJ Open Forum* 2024 (scoping review; 309 records, 13–14 studies; 11 bariatric, 2 Ozempic opinion pieces, 1 objective GLP-1 dataset). Conclusion: massive weight loss causes apparent accelerated facial aging through fat devolumisation and more skin laxity. The authors say **gradual loss has not been studied**; most included cohorts lost weight rapidly after bariatric surgery. Samples were mostly Caucasian with short follow-up. Verified. — [Jafar et al. 2024, ASJ Open Forum](https://academic.oup.com/asjopenforum/article/doi/10.1093/asjof/ojae069/7739023)
  - Couto (n=7, bariatric massive loss): massive-loss patients looked **5.1 years older** than their actual age, vs **1.2 years** for non-massive loss. — same source
  - Valente (bariatric, prospective): mean perceived facial age rose from **40.8 to 43.7** after surgery. — same source
  - Narasimhan (n=22, bariatric, BMI ~38 → 26–27): 88 % had midface/nasolabial volume loss, 82 % platysma bands, ~60 % perioral volume loss. — same source
  - Papoian (n=14, bariatric): **mild** loss was linked to looking *younger* but less attractive. **Substantial** loss was linked to looking *older* but *more* attractive. — same source
  - Deot (conference abstract, n=5 on Ozempic, all Caucasian, 4:1 female): 21.1 % weight loss over 9.4 months. Superficial temporal fat pad fell **41.8 %** and superficial cheek fat **69.9 %**. Only two regions measured, not peer-reviewed as a full paper. — same source
  - Some authors suggest the **buccal fat pad resists** weight-loss change (robust blood supply). No study has examined this in younger patients. — same source
  - Patients **over 40** were more susceptible to weight-loss-related facial volume change (Valente). — same source
- Bariatric facial 3D surface-imaging study (*J Cranio-Maxillofacial Surgery*): facial changes after massive weight loss **vary by facial region**, and there was **no correlation between total weight lost and cervical (neck) volume loss**. Abstract only. — [Morphological changes of the face after massive weight loss, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1010518220301311)

**Other 2024–2025 reviews and surveys**
- Sciscent et al., *Facial Plastic Surgery & Aesthetic Medicine* 2025, argue the effect follows **weight loss generally, not the drug specifically**. They name loss of deep medial cheek fat and deflation of buccal and temporal fat pads, and note a similar look after bariatric surgery. Secondary only (search summary of the abstract). — [Sciscent et al. 2025, FPSAM](https://doi.org/10.1089/fpsam.2024.0083)
- Montecinos, *Dermatological Reviews* 2024: a narrative review describing a prematurely aged face after rapid semaglutide loss. No new data. Secondary only. — [Montecinos 2024, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/der2.70003)
- A *J Cosmetic Dermatology* survey of 504 GLP-1 users found that, among those reporting skin changes, 10.9 % reported facial volume loss, 8.3 % sagging and 7.7 % jowling. **UNVERIFIED**: the figure came through a search summary and the primary paper was not located.
- Searches for facial-volume procedures rose alongside "Ozempic face" searches (Google Trends). This shows interest, not incidence. — [Google Trends analysis, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12622603/)

**Facial anatomy and aging without weight loss**
- A 2026 3D-MRI study (*ASJ Open Forum*; 47 women aged 18–65, cross-sectional) found **buccal fat pad and nasolabial fold fat volumes decreased with age**, while upper-face superficial fat stayed stable. It shows that age-related deep and midface fat loss happens on its own. — [Age-related facial fat changes using 3D MRI, PubMed 41928780](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41928780/)
- An ongoing trial (NCT07419854) is measuring facial volume restoration after GLP-1 loss using 3D photography. Prospective facial-volume data are still being generated. — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07419854](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07419854)

**Twin and perception studies (landmark, older)**
- Guyuron et al., *Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery* 2009 (landmark; 186 identical twin pairs, panel-rated photos). A **4-point higher BMI made a twin look older under age 40, but younger after 40**. An 8-point difference had its cutoff at age 55. Longer smoking made twins look older. — [ScienceDaily summary of Guyuron 2009](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/02/090203110511.htm); [ASPS press release](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/press-releases/divorce-antidepressants-or-weight-gainloss-can-add-years-to-your-face); [paper PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/longevity/2009-guyuron.pdf)
- Coetzee, Perrett & Stephen 2009 (*Perception*): perceived facial adiposity predicts perceived **health and attractiveness**, and normal-weight faces were rated healthiest and most attractive. — [St Andrews press release](https://news.st-andrews.ac.uk/archive/weight-matters/); [Bournemouth record, "Facial adiposity: a cue to health?"](https://staffprofiles.bournemouth.ac.uk/display/journal-article/388616)
- Re et al.: observers notice BMI changes of about **1.3 kg/m² in men's faces**, so facial weight change is perceptible early in a cut. — [Wie viel Gewicht liegt im Gesicht? St Andrews portal](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/wie-viel-gewicht-liegt-im-gesicht-wahrnehmungsschwellen-f%C3%BCr-k%C3%B6rpe/)

### Inferences
- "Ozempic face" is best described as **facial deflation after substantial weight loss**, not a drug toxicity. Both systematic reviews and the FPSAM review frame it that way. The peer-reviewed signal is strongest in bariatric patients (often 40–50 % of body weight lost, BMI > 35–40).
- For a man under 40 moving from overweight toward a normal-to-lean BMI, the twin data suggest that below 40, *less* facial fat tends to read as *younger*. The Papoian data suggest substantial loss can read as more attractive even when it reads slightly older. Both are older, small, perception-based studies, so treat this as directional only.
- Region matters. Temple and cheek (superficial) fat shrink. The buccal pad may be more resistant. Deep midface fat thins with age regardless of weight.

### Gaps
- **No controlled study measures facial fat by MRI or 3D before and after weight loss comparing GLP-1 with diet-only or bariatric loss.** The only GLP-1 volumetric data are the n=5 Deot abstract.
- No study of facial appearance after **diet-and-exercise loss of about 15–20 % in men aged 30–40**. Bariatric cohorts are mostly women around 40 with massive loss.
- No data on East Asian faces. Every cohort above was predominantly Caucasian.
- The 504-user JCD survey figures could not be verified at the primary source.

## (b) Rate vs total amount lost vs GLP-1-specific; does fat loss "destroy collagen"?

### Takeaway
The evidence points to **total amount lost, age and baseline skin quality** as the determinants. **Rate of loss has never been studied in isolation** for facial outcomes. The claim that "fast fat loss destroys facial collagen" has **no direct human evidence**. The only histology comparison (bariatric vs non-surgical massive loss) found equal collagen content. GLP-1-specific skin mechanisms (adipose-derived stem cells, dermal fat) are cell or animal hypotheses.

### Cited Findings
- Amount: in bariatric cohorts, massive loss was linked to more apparent aging (5.1 vs 1.2 years older) and to roughly twice the fat-grafting need of non-massive loss. — [Jafar et al. 2024](https://academic.oup.com/asjopenforum/article/doi/10.1093/asjof/ojae069/7739023)
- Rate: *"Most studies involved rapid loss… gradual loss has not been studied."* No study isolates rate. — [Jafar et al. 2024](https://academic.oup.com/asjopenforum/article/doi/10.1093/asjof/ojae069/7739023); the 2025 systematic review likewise did not analyse rate. — [Daneshgaran et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232544/)
- Age: facial changes are said to be "more pronounced in older individuals with naturally lower levels of collagen". Patients over 40 were more susceptible. — [Daneshgaran et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232544/); [Jafar et al. 2024](https://academic.oup.com/asjopenforum/article/doi/10.1093/asjof/ojae069/7739023)
- GLP-1 vs other loss: no preferential facial atrophy has been shown for GLP-1 drugs. Moderate drug-assisted loss (typically ≤25 % of body weight) can produce some of the same issues as massive loss. — [Daneshgaran et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232544/)
- For reference on GLP-1 loss rates: STEP-1 semaglutide gave **14.9 % loss over 68 weeks** (vs 2.4 % placebo), with peak loss around 52 weeks. That is roughly 0.2–0.3 %/week on average. — [Daneshgaran et al. 2025, citing Wilding 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232544/). The Deot n=5 Ozempic group lost 21.1 % in 9.4 months (≈0.5 %/week). — [Jafar et al. 2024](https://academic.oup.com/asjopenforum/article/doi/10.1093/asjof/ojae069/7739023)

**Collagen histology after weight loss**
- Hany et al., *Obesity Surgery* 2024 (prospective; 77 patients, skin biopsies from abdominoplasty and breast reduction, surgical vs non-surgical massive loss). **Collagen content did not differ** (abdomen 49.2 % vs 49.5 %, p=0.80; breast 49.3 % vs 49.6 %). Epidermal thickness did not differ either. Elastin was lower only in surgical-loss abdominal skin (5.0 % vs 6.8 %, p=0.029). The authors wrote that "collagen content was equally reduced in both groups", but there was **no non-obese control group**, so "reduced" was judged against literature values. Rate and nutrition were not measured. Longer time since loss was associated with more elastin (estimate 0.17, p=0.016). Verified. — [Hany et al. 2024, Obes Surg (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/10899414)

**GLP-1, dermal fat and stem-cell mechanisms (hypothesis level)**
- Paschou et al., *Endocrine* 2025 (narrative review, "GLP-1RA and the possible skin aging") states that *"Studies on the direct effects of GLP-1RA on skin aging are limited."* It cites **no human skin study of GLP-1RAs**. The claimed pathways rest on cell-culture work (liraglutide on human adipose-derived stem cells, Cantini 2015) and on animal or non-skin studies:
  - reduced ADSC proliferation
  - lower local estrogen leading to less collagen
  - AGE reduction.
  It calls "Ozempic face" "an outcome of rapid weight loss and malnutrition", but **malnutrition is asserted, not measured**. It does not discuss protein, vitamin C or zinc. Verified. — [Paschou et al. 2025, Endocrine (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12370548/)
- Ridha et al., *Aesthetic Surgery Journal* 2024 (review) propose loss of dermal and subcutaneous fat and altered ADSC behaviour as drivers, and recommend fat grafting or fillers. This is mechanistic and hypothesis-level. Secondary only. — [Ridha et al. 2024, ASJ](https://academic.oup.com/asj/article/44/11/NP809/7687729)
- Žaliukaitė & Lebbar, *J Clin Med* 2026: GLP-1 receptors are expressed in skin, but their role in skin aging is "insufficiently clarified". Secondary only. — [Žaliukaitė & Lebbar 2026 (VU repository PDF)](https://epublications.vu.lt/object/elaba:286715606/286715606.pdf)
- The 2025 systematic review describes volume loss "without compensatory collagen remodeling" as the likely cause, and flags this as **not experimentally shown**. — [Daneshgaran et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232544/)

### Inferences
- The viral claim that "fast fat loss destroys facial collagen" is **not supported**. No study measured facial dermal collagen against rate of loss. The one biopsy comparison found equal collagen after two different routes of massive loss. The ADSC and dermal-fat story is plausible biology, but it has been shown only in cells and animals, for the drug, not for diet-induced loss.
- In this person's case, his **rate (~1 %/week for 15 weeks) is faster than typical semaglutide loss** (~0.2–0.5 %/week). "Not being on GLP-1" therefore offers no protection on rate grounds, if rate matters. But no evidence says rate matters independently of amount. His **total planned loss (~19–22 % of starting weight)** sits in the GLP-1 range, well below bariatric "massive loss". Being under 40 is in his favour on the available data.
- The plausible reason faster loss *looks* worse is simple: the face deflates before the skin has had time to contract, so the drop is more visible over a short span. The 2024 histology hint that elastin improves with time since loss supports this, but it is weak, indirect evidence.

### Gaps
- No study separates rate from amount for facial or skin outcomes in any population.
- No human study of dermal collagen in diet-only loss of 10–25 %.
- No human study shows GLP-1 drugs reduce skin collagen. Every mechanistic claim is preclinical.

## (c) Collagen inputs: low protein, energy deficit and micronutrients (vitamin C, zinc, copper)

### Takeaway
Vitamin C is a required cofactor for collagen hydroxylation, and clinical skin disease from protein-energy or zinc deficiency is well described. But those are frank deficiency states, such as hospital malnutrition. **No evidence was found that a planned cut with adequate protein (~1.9 g/kg) impairs skin collagen.** Evidence that extra dietary vitamin C above adequacy reduces wrinkling is unsettled.

### Cited Findings
- Vitamin C is required for collagen hydroxylation (stability). In cell culture it stabilises collagen mRNA and raises collagen synthesis. The Linus Pauling Institute notes the effects of vitamin C in skin "are not well understood due to limited research". — [Linus Pauling Institute, Vitamin C and Skin Health](https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/health-disease/skin-health/vitamin-C)
- Oral absorption of vitamin C into skin is limited, which is why dermatology prefers topical forms. A 2022 review notes variable bioavailability and formulation instability. — [Ascorbic acid as a cosmeceutical, PMC9495646](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9495646/)
- Protein-energy malnutrition (marasmus or kwashiorkor) causes xerosis, atrophy and desquamation. Zinc deficiency gives acral and periorificial crusted dermatitis, angular cheilitis and delayed wound healing. Both are described in catabolic, hospitalised patients. — [Nutritional Dermatoses in the Hospitalized Patient, MDedge/Cutis](https://mdedge.com/content/nutritional-dermatoses-hospitalized-patient)

### Inferences
- With ~1.9 g/kg protein and plenty of vegetables (vitamin C sources such as capsicum, broccoli and leafy greens), a frank collagen-synthesis deficit is unlikely. This is an inference: nothing measured his skin or his levels.
- His dry, peeling, itchy skin since the cut has **no direct literature tying it to calorie restriction**. It is worth thinking about the differential, though:
  - seasonal or environmental dryness
  - asteatotic eczema
  - irritation from sunscreen or soap
  - lower dietary fat.
  The MDedge review lists asteatotic eczema (dry skin with superficial fissuring) as a key look-alike of nutritional dermatoses. If it persists despite moisturiser, or shows acral, periorificial or angular-cheilitis features (zinc pattern), that would justify a GP or dermatologist review and bloodwork rather than supplements.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on skin barrier or xerosis in healthy adults during moderate calorie restriction.
- No data on copper and collagen at normal intakes.
- No NHANES-style dietary vitamin C vs wrinkling analysis was retrieved. A Cosgrove 2007 AJCN analysis is commonly cited for this, but it was **not verified** here.

## (d) Loose skin and elasticity after weight loss: what determines it

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed data on loose-skin determinants are thin. The factors repeated across reviews are the **amount lost, age, duration of obesity, sun damage and smoking**, with genetics assumed. Most quantitative claims online come from clinic blogs.

### Cited Findings
- Massive loss (bariatric) is linked to skin laxity and vertical sagging of the face and neck (Peters), submental skin and jowl laxity (Sclafani 2005), and neck skin and chin ptosis (Biörserud). — [Jafar et al. 2024](https://academic.oup.com/asjopenforum/article/doi/10.1093/asjof/ojae069/7739023)
- In the 2024 biopsy study, elastin (not collagen) was the fibre that differed. Elastin proportion **rose with time since weight loss**, which suggests some remodelling over time. Weak evidence. — [Hany et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/10899414)
- Older people are more susceptible to weight-loss facial changes. — [Jafar et al. 2024](https://academic.oup.com/asjopenforum/article/doi/10.1093/asjof/ojae069/7739023)
- Consumer and clinic sources list duration of excess weight, ≥46 kg lost, rapid loss, age, smoking, UV and genetics, but cite no primary data. **Treat as unverified opinion.** — [Healthline, loose skin after weight loss](https://www.healthline.com:443/nutrition/loose-skin-after-weight-loss)

### Inferences
- He has favourable factors on this list: age 36, about 14 kg lost (not ≥46 kg), a short time at a higher weight (unknown), and he does not smoke (assumed; not stated in the brief). His unfavourable factor is **high cumulative UV** (Perth).

### Gaps
- No quantitative model of loose-skin risk by age, amount or rate exists in the peer-reviewed sources found.
- No facial-specific laxity data for moderate loss.

## (e) What ages skin most (UV, smoking, glycation, sleep, other) and specifics for East Asian skin

### Takeaway
UV is the dominant modifiable driver of visible facial aging. The widely quoted "80 %" is a hedged estimate from one study of French women, but the direction is robust. Smoking is the next best-established factor. Glycation and sleep have mechanistic or small-study support. In East Asian skin, **pigmentation** (spots, uneven tone) is a prominent photoaging feature, but wrinkling also matters.

### Cited Findings
- Flament et al. 2013 (*Clin Cosmet Investig Dermatol*; 298 Caucasian women, France): UV "**seems to be** responsible for 80 % of visible facial aging signs". Pigmentation disorders were strongly UV-linked. **Ptosis (sagging) was linked to chronological aging and did not differ by sun exposure.** Wrinkles and texture were affected by both. Landmark. — [Flament et al. 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24101874/); [full text, Dovepress](https://www.dovepress.com/effect-of-the-sun-on-visible-clinical-signs-of-aging-in-caucasian-skin-peer-reviewed-article-CCID)
- Smoking: in the twin study, longer smoking meant an older appearance. — [ScienceDaily, Guyuron 2009](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/02/090203110511.htm). In 407 Koreans, smoking, sun exposure and female sex independently predicted wrinkles, but smoking did not predict dyspigmentation (Chung 2001, landmark). — [Photoaging in Asians, PubMed 12914595](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12914595)
- East Asian skin: in 1,081 ethnic Chinese young adults (Singapore/Malaysia, published 2024), the Korean photo-numeric scale's **dyspigmentation component** captured Chinese photoaging well, and wrinkle scales gave near-identical results across scales. In a separate cohort of 3,281 ethnic Chinese people, wrinkles, pigmentation and photo-ageing explained only ~24 % of variance in aging phenotypes. — [Singapore/Malaysia SMCGES cohort, PMC11116842](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11116842/)
- Glycation: AGEs accumulate in collagen and elastin and stiffen them. Grilling, frying and roasting produce more dietary AGEs than boiling or steaming. UV intensifies AGE accumulation. Human evidence linking dietary sugar specifically to skin aging is thin. — ["Sugar Sag", PubMed 27224842](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27224842/); [anti-glycation agents review, PMC12024170](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12024170/)
- Sleep: Oyetakin-White et al. 2015 (60 women aged 30–49, **Estée Lauder-commissioned**). Poor sleepers had higher intrinsic-aging (SCINEXA) scores, **4.4 vs 2.2**, with no difference in extrinsic aging. Barrier recovery after tape-stripping was 30 % better in good sleepers (+14 % vs −6 %). **Industry-funded and small. Figures secondary only.** — [IJMS 2016 review table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4881480/table/ijms-17-00654-t002); [Newswise press release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/est-e-lauder-clinical-trial-finds-link-between-sleep-deprivation-and-skin-aging)

### Inferences
- In Perth, UV outweighs everything else on this list for visible facial aging, and especially for pigmentation in East Asian skin. By comparison, the cut's effect on the face is mostly volume (ptosis-like), which Flament found was *not* sun-driven.
- Alcohol, stress and pollution were not researched in depth here (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No 2023+ primary studies on alcohol, psychological stress or air pollution and facial aging were retrieved in this pass.
- The "80 % from UV" figure is from Caucasian women. No equivalent estimate for East Asian men was found.
- No verified 2023+ review specifically on East Asian photoaging was found.

## (f) Prevention with evidence: sunscreen, retinoids, vitamin C, moisturiser, diet, sleep, resistance training, collagen supplements

### Takeaway
The two best-evidenced interventions are **daily broad-spectrum sunscreen**, which has RCT evidence of slower skin aging, and **topical retinoids**, with tretinoin supported by meta-analysis. Resistance training has one small but interesting 2023 RCT-like study showing increased dermal thickness. **Collagen peptide supplements show benefit only in industry-funded and low-quality trials**, per a 2025 meta-analysis. In his case they are excluded anyway by the hepatitis B no-supplement rule.

### Cited Findings
**Sunscreen**
- Hughes et al., *Annals of Internal Medicine* 2013 (landmark RCT, Nambour, Queensland; 903 adults under 55; 4.5 years). **Daily** sunscreen users showed significantly less skin aging (hand skin-surface microtopography) than discretionary users. Beta-carotene supplementation had **no** effect. The commonly quoted figure is "**24 % less skin aging**". Secondary only: the primary results table was not read. — [MDedge summary](https://mdedge.com/familymedicine/article/59024/dermatology/daily-sunscreen-slowed-skin-aging-middle-aged-adults); [QxMD record, PMID 23732711](https://read.qxmd.com/read/23732711/sunscreen-and-prevention-of-skin-aging-a-randomized-trial); [AsianScientist (24 % figure)](https://www.asianscientist.com/?p=35193)
- Cancer Council Australia: apply sunscreen **every day the UV Index is forecast to reach 3 or above** (the ICNIRP skin-damage threshold). Use **SPF50/50+, broad-spectrum, water-resistant**, applied 20 minutes before going out and **reapplied every 2 hours**. Combine it with clothing, hat, shade and sunglasses. This matches the ANZ consensus statement. — [Cancer Council, preventing skin cancer with sunscreen](https://www.cancer.org.au/cancer-information/causes-and-prevention/sun-safety/preventing-skin-cancer-with-sunscreen); [Cancer Council, sunscreen basics](https://www.cancer.org.au/cancer-information/causes-and-prevention/sun-safety/sunscreen/basics); [ANZ consensus statement, PubMed 30681231](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30681231/)

**Topical retinoids**
- Tretinoin meta-analysis (8 RCTs, 1,361 patients, searched to Jan 2024): tretinoin significantly improved facial photodamage, including fine and coarse wrinkles, vs vehicle. Safe and effective. — [Tretinoin for photodamaged facial skin, DPC journal](https://www.dpcj.org/index.php/dpc/article/view/5172)
- A 2022 systematic review (7 RCTs) found tretinoin safe and well tolerated. — [Topical tretinoin for photoaging, PubMed 35620028](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35620028/)
- A 2024 review calls tretinoin the "gold standard" topical anti-aging agent. — [Comparing tretinoin to other topicals, PubMed 39348007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39348007/)
- 2025 network meta-analysis (*Scientific Reports*; 23 RCTs, 3,905 participants): isotretinoin, **retinol** and tretinoin all improved fine wrinkles. It flags **potential commercial bias** and limited racial diversity. — [2025 NMA, PMC12289910](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12289910); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/20bc62c7ce564088ba144fb538a44061)

**Topical vitamin C**
- Topical vitamin C has more clinical support than oral, but evidence is limited by formulation instability and heterogeneous studies. — [PMC9495646](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9495646/); [Linus Pauling Institute](https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/health-disease/skin-health/vitamin-C)

**Resistance training**
- Nishikori et al., *Scientific Reports* 2023;13:10214 (61 sedentary middle-aged **Japanese women**, 56 analysed; 16 weeks, twice weekly, aerobic vs resistance training). **Both** improved skin elasticity and upper-dermal structure. **Only resistance training increased dermal thickness**, linked to lower circulating CCL28, CXCL4 and N,N-dimethylglycine and higher dermal biglycan. Small, short, women only, no non-exercise control (per summaries). Mechanism unconfirmed. — [DOAJ record, Sci Rep 2023](https://doaj.org/article/de7ddeac0d714b5e80b451ca851333a2); [Dermatology Times](https://dermatologytimes.com/view/are-aerobics-or-weights-better-for-aging-skin-); [Lifespan.io](https://www.lifespan.io/news/aerobic-resistance-training-differently-affect-skin-aging/)

**Collagen supplements (reported only; not recommended under his hepatitis B rule)**
- Myung & Park, *American Journal of Medicine* 2025 (23 RCTs, 1,474 participants). The pooled analysis appeared positive. But **non-industry-funded trials showed no effect** on hydration, elasticity or wrinkles, while industry-funded trials did. **High-quality trials showed no effect**, and low-quality trials showed elasticity gains. Conclusion: "currently no clinical evidence to support the use of collagen supplements to prevent or treat skin aging." Secondary only (trade press). — [NutraIngredients, industry reaction to the meta-analysis](https://nutraingredients.com/Article/2025/08/26/industry-reacts-to-meta-analysis-concluding-collagen-supplements-show-no-proven-benefit-for-skin-aging)
- Industry pushback (BioCell Technology, Collagen Stewardship Alliance) argued the review ignored formulation diversity. These are interested parties. — [CosmeticsDesign](https://www.cosmeticsdesign.com/Article/2025/08/26/industry-reacts-to-meta-analysis-concluding-collagen-supplements-show-no-proven-benefit-for-skin-aging)

**Diet and sleep**
- Beta-carotene supplements did not slow skin aging in the Nambour RCT. — [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/familymedicine/article/59024/dermatology/daily-sunscreen-slowed-skin-aging-middle-aged-adults)
- Steaming or boiling rather than grilling or frying lowers dietary AGEs (mechanistic). — [PubMed 27224842](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27224842/)
- Sleep quality was associated with less intrinsic aging and better barrier recovery (small, industry-funded). — [PMC4881480 table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4881480/table/ijms-17-00654-t002)

### Inferences
- In his situation, the measures that matter most, in order of evidence:
  1. **SPF50+ every day, not "most mornings"**, plus reapplication and a hat in Perth sun. Under Cancer Council guidance, UV ≥3 covers most days of the year in Perth.
  2. A topical retinoid. Tretinoin needs a prescription in Australia; retinol is OTC with weaker evidence. Retinoids can worsen dryness and peeling, so this needs sequencing, ideally after the current xerosis settles or under a dermatologist's guidance.
  3. Continued resistance training.
  4. Sleep.
  5. Slowing the rate as he approaches target. This is a reasonable precaution, but it is not evidence-based for the face specifically.
- None of these require new oral supplements.
- **What can't be reversed vs can.** Volume loss from fat reduction is reversible only by regaining fat or by fillers or fat grafting. Facial plastic-surgery reviews advise **at least 6 months of stable weight before elective facial procedures**. — [Daneshgaran et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232544/). Photoaging pigmentation and fine wrinkles respond partly to retinoids and sun protection. Sagging (ptosis) tracks chronological age and is the least reversible by topicals (Flament). — [Flament 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24101874/)
- **When to see a dermatologist (inference, not guideline):**
  - dryness, peeling or itch that persists after 2–4 weeks of consistent moisturising
  - fissuring, rash, or periorificial or acral lesions (possible deficiency pattern)
  - wanting prescription tretinoin
  - new or changing pigmented spots. In Perth this needs a skin check regardless of aging concerns.
  - after reaching and holding goal weight for about 6 months, if facial volume concerns persist.

### Gaps
- No 2023+ RCT of sunscreen on facial aging in East Asian men was found. A 2016 one-year SPF30 facial study (Randhawa, industry-funded) exists but was not verified here.
- No evidence was found on whether resistance training changes *facial* fat or volume appearance. Nishikori measured skin, not face shape, and only in women.
- Moisturiser-specific anti-aging RCTs were not retrieved.
- The primary text of Myung & Park 2025 was not read, so subgroup sizes are unverified.

# Immune function, resistance to viral infection, and chronic hepatitis B in the context of lifestyle

Scope note: the notes are for a 36-year-old Taiwanese man in Perth with chronic hepatitis B (CHB), described as controlled, bloodwork twice a year and all normal, antiviral status unknown. He has been in a fat-loss cut since 2026-07-15 (102 to about 88 kg, recently 0.75-1 %/week, about 2,520 kcal, protein about 1.9 g/kg), trains strength 5 days/week plus a daily treadmill, sleeps 6-7 h, and travels to Taiwan Jan-Feb 2027. Everything clinical below is framed as "ask the doctor". Nothing here recommends a new supplement. Sources were checked online on 2026-10-25 unless marked otherwise. Older landmark studies are labelled "(landmark, pre-2023)".

## 1. Calorie deficit and immunity: does a moderate deficit impair immune defences?

### Takeaway
The best human trial of a moderate deficit (CALERIE 2, about 14 % calorie restriction for 2 years) found immune *benefits*: more functional thymus and less inflammation, not impairment. The harm signal comes from *low energy availability*: deficits that are severe, prolonged, or paired with heavy training and low carbohydrate (IOC REDs 2023). The person's current rate (0.75-1 %/week, about 2,520 kcal, high protein) sits in the moderate band. Running the cut for a long time while training hard and sleeping 6-7 h is the scenario to watch.

### Cited Findings
- CALERIE 2 (landmark, 2022): healthy adults asked to cut intake by 14 % for 2 years showed, on MRI, less fat and more functional volume in the thymus, while controls did not change. The gene for PLA2G7, a macrophage protein linked to inflammation, was suppressed with calorie restriction. In mice, lowering PLA2G7 preserved thymus function and reduced age-related inflammation via the NLRP3 inflammasome. Spadaro O et al., *Science* 2022;375:671-677, doi:10.1126/science.abg7292 — [Yale News summary](https://news.yale.edu/2022/02/10/calorie-restriction-trial-reveals-key-factors-enhancing-human-health); [News-Medical summary](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20220211/Yale-researchers-confirm-the-health-benefits-of-moderate-calorie-restrictions-in-humans.aspx). Note: only press summaries were read, not the paper.
- The IOC 2023 consensus on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) lists **immunity** among the systems harmed by problematic low energy availability (LEA), together with energy metabolism, reproduction, bone, glycogen synthesis, and cardiovascular and haematological health — [Mountjoy M et al., BJSM 2023;57:1073-1097, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994 (PDF)](https://appliedsportpsych.org/site/assets/files/1113/mountjoy_et_al_-_2023_reds_update.pdf); [Amsterdam UMC record](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/en/publications/2023-international-olympic-committees-ioc-consensus-statement-on-/)
- The consensus puts LEA on a spectrum from **adaptable** (brief, mild) to **problematic** (prolonged or severe). REDs becomes a concern when exposure to the problematic range is too long or too severe — [News-Medical on the IOC statement](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20230926/Prolonged-mismatch-between-calories-eaten-and-burned-may-be-putting-many-athletes-at-risk-of-REDs.aspx)
- Low **carbohydrate** availability is described as accelerating REDs and as being linked to lowered immunity and depleted iron, sometimes even without LEA — [Human Performance Alliance summary of IOC 2023](https://humanperformancealliance.org/news/2023-international-olympic-committee-consensus-statement-on-reds/)
- Vitamin D: a 2021 meta-analysis of 37 RCTs found a small protective effect of vitamin D supplementation against acute respiratory infection (OR 0.92, 95 % CI 0.86-0.99). The 2025 update (43 RCTs, more than 49,000 participants) found a slightly smaller effect whose CI overlaps the null. Benefit concentrated in daily 400-1,000 IU dosing — [Jolliffe DA et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2021;9:276-292 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33798465/); [2025 update summary, McMaster Optimal Aging](https://www.mcmasteroptimalaging.org/full-article/PLUS/vitamin-supplementation-prevent-acute-respiratory-infections-systematic-review-115934). This is context only: he already takes a multivitamin, and the rule is no new supplements.

### Inferences
- A deficit producing 0.75-1 %/week with protein of about 1.9 g/kg is closer to CALERIE-style moderate restriction than to REDs. The main risk factors in REDs are **duration** and **severity**, compounded by high training volume (5 strength days plus daily treadmill) and low carbohydrate. Planned diet breaks and keeping carbohydrate adequate around training are the levers consistent with the IOC model.
- CALERIE participants were non-obese, not training heavily, and losing weight slowly (about 10 % body weight over 2 years). A faster cut on top of heavy training is a different exposure, so CALERIE shows that a moderate deficit is not inherently immunosuppressive. It does not show that every deficit is safe.

### Gaps
- No 2023-2026 study directly quantifying upper respiratory infection (URTI) risk versus *rate* of weight loss in non-athlete adults on moderate cuts was found.
- The IOC statement's immune-specific evidence section (infection rates, mechanisms) was not read in full; the immunity point comes from its abstract and summaries.
- Zinc and iron and infection risk (beyond the IOC mention of iron depletion) were not researched in this pass; no source collected.

## 2. Exercise immunology: is the "open window" still accepted; moderate vs very heavy loads

### Takeaway
Regular moderate-to-vigorous activity is consistently linked to less severe COVID-19 (about 40-50 % lower hospitalisation and death in meta-analyses), with benefit plateauing around 150-300 min/week of moderate activity. The "open window" idea (that a single hard session suppresses immunity for hours) has been strongly challenged since 2018. Infection risk is still raised during periods of very heavy training and after marathon-type events, especially when combined with poor sleep, stress and low energy intake.

### Cited Findings
- Campbell and Turner (landmark, 2018) argue there is limited reliable evidence that vigorous exercise raises opportunistic infection risk. They reinterpret the post-exercise drop in blood lymphocytes 1-2 h after exercise as immune cells **redistributing** to peripheral tissues (immune surveillance), not suppression. They also argue that frequent exercise improves immune competence and may slow immune ageing — [Campbell JP, Turner JE, Front Immunol 2018;9:648](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5911985/)
- A 2023 Springer chapter "Exercise and immunity: Beliefs and facts" critically reviews the J-curve and open-window hypotheses — [record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4087759?lng=en). Only the abstract was available, so its conclusions are unverified.
- Rahmati et al. (2022, 12 studies, about 1.26 million patients): compared with inactive people, physically active people had lower COVID-19 hospitalisation (RR 0.58, 95 % CI 0.46-0.73), ICU admission (RR 0.65) and death (RR 0.47) — [news summary of Rahmati, Rev Med Virol 2022](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20220823/Analysis-finds-link-between-regular-physical-activity-and-lower-risk-of-COVID-19-severity.aspx)
- Ezzatvar et al. (BJSM 2022, 16 studies, 1,853,610 adults): regular activity was linked to lower risk of infection, hospitalisation, severe illness and death. The dose-response plateaued around **500 MET-min/week** (roughly 150-300 min/week of moderate activity). Authors flag heterogeneity and possible publication bias — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2022/07/07/bjsports-2022-105733); [Hospital Healthcare summary](https://hospitalhealthcare.com/specialisms/greater-regular-physical-activity-reduces-risk-of-adverse-covid-19-outcomes/)
- Sittichai et al. (2022): ≥150 min/week of moderate or ≥75 min/week of vigorous activity reduced severe COVID-19 and mortality. A Mendelian-randomisation study found no significant effect of moderate-to-vigorous activity, though light activity lowered hospitalisation — [Frontiers in Physiology 2023 paper citing both](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1293229/full)
- Heavy loads: endurance athletes have higher URTI risk during periods of heavy training and in the 1-2 weeks after race events, but most do not get ill. The pattern is J-shaped, with moderate training lowering risk and overreaching or overtraining raising it — [Nieman DC (landmark, 2000), "Is infection risk linked to exercise workload?" Med Sci Sports Exerc](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10910297/)
- LA Marathon data (landmark): runners were more likely to fall ill in the week after the race than non-participants, with higher risk in those training more than 60 miles/week — [The Scientist, 2024](https://www.the-scientist.com/why-do-some-runners-get-sick-after-a-marathon-73060)
- Countermeasures named in a 2021 review: correct nutrition, sleep hygiene, sensible organisation of training loads, and stress management — [Cicchella A et al., "URTI in sport and the immune system response", 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8146667/)

### Inferences
- His training (strength 5 days plus daily treadmill) is well above the 150-300 min/week COVID-severity plateau, so more exercise adds no immune benefit. The question is whether total load plus the deficit plus 6-7 h sleep pushes toward the heavy-load end of the J-curve. Watching resting HR, HRV and Body Battery trends (already in the Garmin pipeline) is a reasonable proxy for overreaching. That is an inference, not a validated immune marker.
- The debate has shifted from "one hard session opens a window" to "chronic overload plus other stressors (sleep, energy, travel, crowds) raise risk". The long-haul flight and crowds in Taiwan are a stacked-stressor moment.

### Gaps
- No 2023-2026 consensus paper specifically declaring the open window refuted was retrieved. Simpson RJ et al., "Can exercise affect immune function to increase susceptibility to infection?" (Exerc Immunol Rev 2020) is the known consensus-style debate paper, but it was **not verified** in this session.
- No 2023+ Nieman review on heavy training and URTI was retrieved.

## 3. Sleep and immunity

### Takeaway
Short sleep is one of the best-replicated lifestyle risk factors for catching a cold and for weaker vaccine antibody responses. Under 6 h a night carried about 4 times the cold risk of more than 7 h in a viral-challenge study. Objectively short sleep blunts vaccine antibody responses, with a large effect in men, and the vaccines studied included hepatitis A and B. At 6-7 h he sits between the high-risk and reference groups.

### Cited Findings
- Prather et al. (landmark, 2015): 164 healthy adults aged 18-55 had sleep measured by wrist actigraphy for 7 nights, were given rhinovirus (RV39) nasal drops and were quarantined for 5 days. Cold risk was **4.2x** higher for <6 h/night and **4.5x** for ≤5 h/night versus >7 h, adjusted for season, BMI, income, education, stress, smoking, exercise and alcohol — [Prather AA et al., Sleep 2015;38(9):1353-1359 (CMU release)](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2015/august/sleep-and-sickness.html); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/infectious-disease/20151014/sleep-duration-influences-infection-susceptibility)
- Spiegel et al. (Current Biology 2023) meta-analysis of 7 studies of influenza and hepatitis A and B vaccines: **objectively measured** short sleep was linked to a robust decrease in antibody response (pooled effect size 0.79, n=304). Self-reported <6 h did not reach significance (ES 0.29). The effect was large and significant in **men** (ES 0.93, 95 % CI 0.54-1.33) but not in women. The authors suggest adequate sleep around vaccination may boost and prolong antibody responses — [Spiegel K et al., Curr Biol 2023;33(5):998-1005.e2, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2023.02.017 (Lübeck record)](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-associations-between-insufficient-sleep-du/); [HealthDay summary](https://consumer.healthday.com/covid-vaccine-2659560960.html)
- The 2021 URTI-in-sport review lists sleep hygiene among the main URTI countermeasures — [Cicchella et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8146667/)

### Inferences
- Getting reliably to 7 h or more, especially in the nights around any vaccination (flu or COVID before the Taiwan trip) and around the flights, is the highest-yield, zero-supplement immune lever in this list. Garmin sleep data can track it objectively, which matters because the Spiegel effect was seen with *objective*, not self-reported, sleep.
- Sleep loss during a deficit also worsens appetite and recovery, but that is outside this note's scope.

### Gaps
- No 2023-2026 viral-challenge replication of Prather was found.
- The exact dose (hours) needed around vaccination is undefined; the Spiegel authors say so explicitly.

## 4. Obesity, visceral fat and immune function: does fat loss help?

### Takeaway
Obesity is linked to impaired innate and adaptive immunity, more infections and faster waning of COVID vaccine protection. Evidence that *losing* fat restores immune function in humans is thin: mostly small, correlational studies.

### Cited Findings
- Described immune changes in obesity: lower cytokine production, reduced response to antigen or mitogen stimulation, reduced macrophage and dendritic-cell function, and natural-killer-cell impairment, with increased susceptibility to several pathogens — [Frontiers in Endocrinology 2022 review (PDF)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2022.898810/pdf)
- Obesity is linked to faster loss of immunity after COVID-19 vaccination, and severe breakthrough disease rose earlier in people with obesity than in normal-weight people (Nature Medicine study) — [RACGP newsGP](https://www1.racgp.org.au/newsgp/clinical/obesity-found-to-speed-up-loss-of-immunity-from-co)
- Small study: 21 patients with morbid obesity on a very-low-carbohydrate diet around their mRNA COVID vaccine doses lost about 10 % of body weight. Higher baseline BMI correlated with a poorer response, and weight loss correlated with higher IFN-γ. The authors say weight loss "may reverse" the effect, but the design is correlational — [Frontiers in Endocrinology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2022.898810/pdf)
- 2026 mouse study (University of Missouri, J Immunol): obesity impaired the quality and durability of antibody responses to a Pseudomonas vaccine. Animal data — [News Tribune, 2026-04-13](https://www.newstribune.com/news/2026/apr/13/mu-researchers-share-findings-about-obesitys-impact-on-vaccines/)
- CALERIE 2 (section 1) is the strongest human evidence that a moderate deficit *improves* immune-ageing markers (thymus function, lower inflammation) — [Yale News](https://news.yale.edu/2022/02/10/calorie-restriction-trial-reveals-key-factors-enhancing-human-health)

### Inferences
- Going from 102 to 88 kg is very likely moving him out of the obesity range, depending on height, which is not given. On the mechanistic and observational evidence, that is more likely to help immune competence than hurt it. No trial proves this.

### Gaps
- No 2023-2026 human RCT showing that fat loss restores vaccine response or reduces infection incidence was found.
- No visceral-fat-specific immune data was collected.

## 5. Chronic hepatitis B: inflammation, flares and what triggers them

### Takeaway
In CHB, liver damage is driven by immune-mediated inflammation (ALT flares) on top of viral replication. The best-documented flare triggers are **immunosuppression** (chemotherapy, rituximab, corticosteroids) and **stopping antiviral therapy (nucleos(t)ide analogues, NAs) without supervision**. If he is on an antiviral, never stopping or skipping it without the hepatologist is the single most important rule, including during travel.

### Cited Findings
- After NA withdrawal, viral reactivation almost always follows, and a proportion of patients have serious acute flares. Occurrence and consequences depend on viral, host and treatment factors — [Hsu YC, Tseng CH, Kao JH, Clin Mol Hepatol 2023;29(4):869-890, doi:10.3350/cmh.2022.0420](https://doaj.org/article/e4d2c47c3a014b8aab83c69b44b79437)
- EASL 2025: a meta-analysis found severe flares or hepatic decompensation in 1.21 % of patients after stopping NAs (3.63 % with cirrhosis, 0.89 % without), with a 0.37 % chance of flare-related death or transplant. NAs should not be stopped in advanced liver disease before HBsAg loss. After stopping tenofovir (TDF), 70 % relapse virologically within 12 weeks, versus <10 % for entecavir in the same period. In pronounced comorbidity or immunosuppression, discontinuation is generally not recommended — [EASL CPG on HBV, J Hepatol 2025, doi:10.1016/j.jhep.2025.03.018 (PDF via hepb.org)](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- HBV reactivation is "not uncommon" during immunosuppressive therapy and can cause flares, decompensation and liver failure. B-cell-depleting agents (rituximab) and corticosteroids are most closely linked — [review in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8795508)
- EASL 2025: immunosuppression can reactivate previously controlled or resolved HBV. For high-risk settings (e.g. B-cell-depleting therapy), NA prophylaxis should continue at least 18 months after immunosuppression ends — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- EASL 2025 on ALT: values ≥40 U/L are generally considered elevated, but lower cut-offs (**30 U/L men**, 19 U/L women) better predict liver-related outcomes. A single normal ALT does not rule out the need for therapy. WHO 2024 also uses ULN 30 U/L for men — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf); [Hepatitis B Foundation simplified WHO 2024 summary](https://www.hepb.org/assets/WHO-Treatment-Guidelines-Final-Simplified-.pdf)
- EASL 2025 treatment indication: antiviral treatment is recommended when ALT is elevated and HBV DNA ≥2,000 IU/mL; all patients with cirrhosis and detectable HBV DNA should be treated. WHO 2024 expanded eligibility, including treatment for significant fibrosis regardless of HBV DNA and for persistently abnormal ALT alone — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf); [WHO 2024 guideline page](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240091368)

### Inferences
- Practical points to raise with the doctor: (1) Is he on an antiviral? If so, bring enough for the whole Taiwan trip plus a buffer and never stop it. (2) Any future steroid or biologic course (even from a different doctor, e.g. for a skin or joint problem) must be flagged as "I have hep B", because reactivation is a known, preventable risk. (3) "Normal ALT" should be read against the 30 U/L male cut-off, not just the lab's flag.
- Common colds and flu are not documented HBV flare triggers in the sources found. The documented triggers are immunosuppression and stopping NAs.

### Gaps
- Evidence on whether intense exercise or a calorie deficit can trigger an HBV flare: none found (no studies located either way).

## 6. MASLD (fatty liver) coexisting with HBV, and how weight loss affects it

### Takeaway
2023-2026 data show a "paradox". Simple hepatic steatosis in CHB is associated with *lower* viral activity and *more* HBsAg loss. **Metabolic dysfunction** (obesity, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, weight gain) is consistently associated with more fibrosis, less fibrosis regression on treatment, more HCC and higher mortality, in a dose-dependent way. The weight loss he is doing addresses exactly the metabolic risk factors that worsen CHB outcomes. No interventional trial proves it changes HBV outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- EASL 2025: data on steatotic liver disease (SLD) in HBV are "the most conflicting". Some show increased HCC; others show lower HCC risk and more spontaneous HBsAg loss. In a study of over 8,700 CHB patients with SLD, **cumulative cardiometabolic risk factors increased all-cause, liver-related and cardiovascular mortality in a dose-dependent manner**, and developing diabetes, hypertension or **weight gain** during follow-up worsened these risks. More than 3 metabolic risk factors and BMI ≥30 are listed as HCC risk factors. Patients should address modifiable factors, including metabolic dysfunction — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- Biopsy cohort (2024): among 1,613 CHB patients, about 30 % had MASLD. Over about 5 years, HCC developed in 4.8 % with MASLD versus 1.2 % without (adjusted HR 3.996, 95 % CI 2.007-7.959) — [Frontiers in Physiology 2024 (PMC10886697)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10886697/)
- Taiwanese cohort (336,866 adults, about 15 years, CGH 2024): adjusted HCC HR 8.86 for MASLD plus CHB/HCV, 8.81 for CHB/HCV without steatosis, and 1.52 for MASLD alone. Among those who got antivirals, MASLD still raised cirrhosis (aHR 1.23) and HCC (aHR 1.32). The virus is the bigger driver, but MASLD adds risk — [Chang Gung University record](https://pure.lib.cgu.edu.tw/en/publications/chronic-viral-hepatitis-b-and-c-outweigh-masld-in-the-associated-/)
- Fibrosis regression (2025, 1,307 CHB patients with paired biopsies, 48-72 weeks on antivirals): metabolic risk factors were associated with **less** fibrosis regression, dose-dependently by number of factors — [summary reported in search results; source: Sonneveld, HBV Forum 2025 slides (PDF)](https://www.forumresearch.org/storage/documents/2025/HBV14/Session4/milan%20sonneveld%20hbv%20forum%20meeting%20final%20%2005052025.pdf). Partially verified: the primary paper was not opened.
- Weight loss and HBsAg loss (Hepat Mon 2024, retrospective, 94 HBsAg-loss patients vs 95 controls): 47 % of the HBsAg-loss group had lost weight beforehand versus 23 % of controls; weight gain was associated with HBsAg persistence. Small, single-centre, association only — [Hepatitis Monthly 2024](https://brieflands.com/journals/hepatmon/articles/142264)
- A 2026 Gut and Liver review frames HBV plus MASLD as a "dual burden with opposing signals": steatosis tracks with lower HBV activity and more HBsAg clearance, metabolic dysfunction with worse liver outcomes — [ReachMD summary](https://reachmd.com/news/metabolic-dysfunction-and-hepatic-steatosis-in-chronic-hepatitis-b/2486856/). Secondary source.
- Australian HCC guideline 2025: people with MASLD without cirrhosis should be monitored for progression to cirrhosis with elastography or similar — [MJA 2025;223(8)](https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2025/223/8/clinical-practice-guidelines-hepatocellular-carcinoma-surveillance-people-high)

### Inferences
- The cut's value for his liver is less about "removing fat from the liver" and more about removing **metabolic risk factors** (weight, insulin resistance, BP, lipids), the component every 2023-2026 source agrees is harmful in CHB. Keeping the weight off (avoiding regain) matters, given EASL's note that weight gain during follow-up worsened outcomes.
- Worth asking the doctor: was steatosis seen on his last ultrasound, and would a FibroScan (which also gives a steatosis score, CAP) be useful as a post-cut baseline?

### Gaps
- No RCT of lifestyle weight loss in CHB with fibrosis or HCC endpoints was found.

## 7. Alcohol and HBV: is any amount safe?

### Takeaway
No source defines a safe level of alcohol for CHB. Risk is dose-dependent: about +6 % cirrhosis and +11.5 % HCC risk per extra 12 g/day (about 1.2 Australian standard drinks of 10 g). EASL lists heavy drinking (≥60 g/day) as an established HCC risk factor. The American College of Gastroenterology advises CHB patients to avoid alcohol. For the Taiwan trip (family meals, toasts), the evidence supports "as little as possible, ideally none". The exact personal limit is a question for his hepatologist.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis (Wu YP et al., J Clin Transl Hepatol, 2024 per EMJ; dated 2025 by the Frontiers review): each additional **12 g/day** of alcohol was associated with **+6.2 % cirrhosis** risk and **+11.5 % HCC** risk in people with HBV, dose-dependent. Heterogeneity and under-reporting limit precision. No safe level stated — [EMJ, 2025-01-23](https://www.emjreviews.com/hepatology/news/alcohols-role-in-progression-of-hbv-related-liver-disease/). Note the year discrepancy between sources.
- Pathania and Osna review (Front Oncol, 2026-02-18): the safe level of alcohol for HBV patients "remains unresolved". The ACG strongly recommends that CHB patients avoid alcohol; most reviewed studies recommend complete abstinence. Drinking >3 units/day is associated with higher odds of cirrhosis (OR 4.3 men). Yun et al. 2023 linked alcohol to liver cancer and mortality in HBV patients **without** cirrhosis — [Frontiers in Oncology 2026, doi:10.3389/fonc.2026.1751474](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2026.1751474/full)
- EASL 2025 Table 8: heavy alcohol intake ≥60 g/day is an HCC risk factor in CHB. Patients should be told to reduce excessive alcohol — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- Taiwanese cirrhosis cohort (2022): >160 vs 80-160 g/day aHR 1.78 for HCC; the ALDH2 rs671 variant (common in East Asians, the "flush" gene) was also linked to HCC risk — [MDedge report](https://mdedge.com/content/alcoholism-and-aldh2-rs671-polymorphism-patients-hbv-related-cirrhosis-necessitate-close)
- Hepatitis Australia: with hepatitis B, reducing or avoiding alcohol lowers the chance of liver damage — [hep.org.au](https://www.hep.org.au/blog/how-alcohol-affects-hepatitis/)
- Australian general-population guideline (not HBV-specific): no more than 10 standard drinks/week and no more than 4 on any day — [NHMRC 2020 guidelines (PDF)](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-07/Australian-guidelines-to-reduce-health-risks-from-drinking-alcohol.pdf)

### Inferences
- The NHMRC "10/week" limit is for healthy adults and should not be read as safe for CHB; the HBV-specific literature points lower.
- If he flushes with alcohol (ALDH2 variant, common in Taiwanese people), that is an extra reason for caution (inference from the ALDH2/HCC link; not individually verified).
- Alcohol also undermines sleep and recovery, compounding sections 2-3 during a trip.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data on occasional light drinking (e.g. a few drinks at a family banquet a handful of times) in non-cirrhotic, well-controlled CHB was found.

## 8. Exercise, coffee and aflatoxin in HBV outcomes

### Takeaway
Physical activity is associated with lower HCC risk in treated CHB in a large Korean cohort, though one 2023 Korean analysis conflicts. Coffee is linked to less fibrosis and HCC in observational HBV data and is named by EASL 2025 as a promising but unproven dietary factor. Aflatoxin (from mouldy peanuts, grains and corn) acts synergistically with HBV to raise HCC risk.

### Cited Findings
- Korean nationwide cohort, 9,727 treatment-naive CHB patients starting entecavir or tenofovir: 1,000-1,500 MET-min/week was associated with lower HCC risk with cirrhosis (aHR 0.61) and without (aHR 0.66) — [Cancers 2021 (MDPI)](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6694/13/14/3424); [PMC8306325](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8306325/)
- Conflicting 2023 signal: in a propensity-matched Korean CHB cohort, regular exercise was associated with a slightly *higher* aHR for HCC (1.08) and liver mortality (1.15), while smoking cessation and alcohol abstinence were protective. Reverse causation or confounding (sicker people told to exercise) is plausible but unverified — [World J Gastroenterol 2023;29(24):3843](https://wjgnet.com/1007-9327/full/v29/i24/3843.htm)
- Coffee in HBV: in the French ANRS CO22 Hepather cohort (treated HBV, cross-sectional), ≥3 cups/day vs none was associated with lower odds of elevated fibrosis scores (APRI OR 0.16, FIB-4 OR 0.35). A Hong Kong case-control study found moderate coffee associated with OR 0.54 for HCC. A small Taiwanese 5-year follow-up found no difference — [HAL record (Hepather)](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03776967); [The Hospitalist summary](https://blogs.the-hospitalist.org/content/does-coffee-help-or-harm-patients-hbv)
- EASL 2025: "dietary factors, particularly coffee consumption, have been linked to hepatic benefits, including protective effects against liver fibrosis and HCC", but nutrition in HBV "has not been systematically evaluated" and needs RCTs. EASL also warns against "unnecessary restrictions due to misconceptions about liver toxicity" — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- Aflatoxin: EASL 2025 says that in high-exposure areas aflatoxin B1 and HBV synergistically increase HCC risk, and reducing aflatoxin exposure could lower HCC cases by 23 %. Air pollution (fine particulate matter) is also listed — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- His training volume (far above 1,000-1,500 MET-min/week) is on the favourable side of the Korean cohort data. Continuing to exercise after the cut ends is liver-relevant, not just body-composition-relevant.
- Coffee: no reason to cut it out for the liver. It is not a proven treatment.
- Aflatoxin in practice: avoid nuts, peanut products, grains or dried foods that look, smell or taste mouldy or stale, a sensible caution in humid climates such as Taiwan. Inference: no source on Taiwan-specific aflatoxin levels was collected.

### Gaps
- No exercise RCT in CHB was found.
- No Taiwan-specific aflatoxin exposure data for 2023-2026 was collected.

## 9. Hepatotoxic supplements and herbal products

### Takeaway
Herbal and dietary supplements are a recognised cause of liver injury, and people with existing liver disease fare worse. Turmeric/curcumin (Australian TGA alert 2023), ashwagandha (2023 Indian series with deaths in people with underlying liver disease) and green tea extract (EGCG ≥800 mg/day raises transaminases; UK COT 2024) are the best-documented in 2023-2026. This supports his "no new supplements" rule. Whether the **Korean ginseng** in his multivitamin is a concern was not resolved here; it is worth listing all current products for the hepatologist.

### Cited Findings
- TGA (Australia, 2023): medicines and supplements containing *Curcuma longa* (turmeric) and/or curcumin may cause liver injury in rare cases. There were 18 reports to 29 June 2023, 9 with enough detail to suggest product-linked injury, and 2 severe (1 fatal). Risk may be higher with **enhanced-absorption/bioavailability** formulations and higher doses. Use "should be avoided in patients with existing or previous liver pathologies". Culinary turmeric in food amounts does not appear to carry the risk — [TGA safety alert](https://www.tga.gov.au/safety/safety-alerts/medicines-containing-turmeric-or-curcumin-risk-liver-injury); [Medicine Today, Sept 2023](https://medicinetoday.com.au/mt/2023/september/in-brief/warning-turmeric-or-curcumincontaining-products). The industry body disputes causality (the fatal case was 82 years old and on naproxen) — [NutraIngredients](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2023/08/29/curcumin-and-liver-injury-risk-correlation-does-not-imply-causation-cma)
- Ashwagandha: an Indian multicentre series (2019-2022) found 23 patients with ashwagandha liver injury, mostly cholestatic. Of 5 with underlying chronic liver disease, 3 developed acute-on-chronic liver failure and **all 3 died**. Chemical analysis found no adulteration, implicating the herb itself — [Philips CA et al., 2023 (PubMed 37756041)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37756041/). An earlier US DILIN/Iceland series (landmark, 2020) reported jaundice after a 2-12-week latency — [PubMed 31991029](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31991029/)
- Green tea extract: EFSA (via UK Committee on Toxicity 2024) found that ≥800 mg/day EGCG as a supplement significantly raises transaminases in RCTs. Hepatotoxicity was reported with one product at 375 mg EGCG. A USP review notes case reports at 140 to about 1,000 mg/day with large individual variability. The weight-loss product Exolise was withdrawn in 2003 after 13 hepatotoxicity cases — [UK COT statement 2024](https://cot.food.gov.uk/Statement%20on%20the%20Hepatotoxicity%20of%20Green%20Tea%20Catechins?print=1); [USP review, PubMed 32140423](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32140423/)

### Inferences
- "Fat burner" and "liver detox" products often contain green tea extract, turmeric or ashwagandha. These are the most likely accidental exposures during a cut or in Taiwanese pharmacies and gift-giving (herbal tonics). The no-new-supplements rule covers them.
- Brewed green tea as a drink is not the same exposure as concentrated extract (inference from the EGCG dose data).

### Gaps
- **Korean ginseng (*Panax ginseng*) hepatotoxicity:** no LiverTox entry or 2023-2026 case series was retrieved in this session. Unresolved; flag for the doctor rather than assume it is safe or unsafe.
- Traditional Chinese herbal medicines (relevant for Taiwan) were not researched specifically.
- Fish oil, CoQ10, creatine and magnesium hepatic safety in CHB were not researched.

## 10. Rapid weight loss and the liver: transient ALT rise, gallstones

### Takeaway
A temporary ALT rise in the early weeks of rapid, very-low-calorie weight loss is common and usually resolves. Gallstone risk climbs steeply above about **1.5 kg/week** or with loss of >25 % of body weight. His current rate (0.75-1 %/week, about 0.65-0.9 kg/week at 88 kg) is below that threshold. Total loss so far (about 14 %) is below 25 %.

### Cited Findings
- Meal-replacement cohort (2020, 105 adults with severe obesity): ALT rose from 28 to 40 IU/L at week 2, then fell to 21 IU/L by week 24 (below baseline). The authors suggest a temporary worsening of steatosis before improvement — [Nutrition & Metabolism 2020](https://nutritionandmetabolism.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12986-020-00512-5)
- Obesity Society report: in a rapid-loss programme, ALT rose in nearly all patients in weeks 3-6 and normalised in 98 % by week 16; described as transient (conference data, not peer-reviewed) — [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/rise-alt-after-weight-loss-no-cause-alarm)
- Gallstones: pooled analysis showed risk rising exponentially with weight-loss rate, steeply above **1.5 kg/week**. The recommended average loss is no more than that. Extremely rapid (>1.5 kg/week) and excessive (>25 % of body weight) loss are the exceptions where weight loss raises, rather than lowers, gallstone risk — [PMC8406364](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8406364/); [Polish Archives of Internal Medicine Q&A](https://www.mp.pl/paim/issue/article/16937/)

### Inferences
- For someone with CHB, an ALT rise during a cut is ambiguous: it could be the benign weight-loss effect or an HBV flare. If routine bloods show raised ALT, tell the doctor about the diet so HBV DNA can be checked to tell the two apart. Do not assume either.
- His rate is in the safe band for gallstones. Prolonged fat-free, very-low-calorie phases would raise risk; his current intake (2,520 kcal) is not one.

### Gaps
- No study of transient ALT during moderate (not very-low-calorie) cuts in CHB patients was found.

## 11. HBV monitoring: what to ask the GP or hepatologist to check, and how often

### Takeaway
EASL 2025 gives explicit intervals for untreated patients. For HBeAg-negative infection with HBV DNA <2,000 IU/mL: ALT and HBV DNA every 12 months, quantitative HBsAg every 12 months, and fibrosis assessment every 2-3 years. Higher HBV DNA or HBeAg-positive means ALT and HBV DNA every 6 months and fibrosis assessment every 12-24 months. Australian 2025 guidance means a **man of Asian background with CHB should be considered for 6-monthly liver ultrasound (± AFP) HCC surveillance from age 40**, earlier with a family history of HCC. At 36, that starts in about 4 years, or now if a first-degree relative had liver cancer.

### Cited Findings
- EASL 2025 (untreated): after a new diagnosis, ALT and HBV DNA every 3-6 months in year 1, then every 6-12 months depending on phase. HBsAg every 12 months. HBeAg/anti-HBe every 12 months if HBeAg-positive. Non-invasive fibrosis assessment at intervals individualised to phase and comorbidities — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- EASL 2025 Table 7 intervals:
  - HBeAg-positive infection: ALT and HBV DNA every 6 months (every 3-6 months if over 30), quantitative HBsAg every 12 months, HBeAg every 6-12 months, fibrosis every 12-24 months.
  - HBeAg-negative with HBV DNA <2,000 IU/mL: ALT and HBV DNA every 12 months, quantitative HBsAg every 12 months, fibrosis every 2-3 years.
  - HBeAg-negative with HBV DNA 2,000-20,000 IU/mL: ALT and HBV DNA every 6 months, fibrosis every 12-24 months, intervals extendable if stable for 3 years or more.
  - On NA therapy: HBV DNA and ALT every 3-6 months until response, then every 6-12 months on entecavir or tenofovir. HBsAg every 12 months. Fibrosis every 12-24 months.
  - EASL notes the evidence for specific intervals is limited (expert consensus).
  — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- EASL 2025 HCC surveillance: recommended for cirrhosis, and for non-cirrhotic patients at intermediate or high risk (e.g. PAGE-B ≥10, family history of HCC, hepatitis delta, advanced fibrosis). HCC risk rises after age 30-35 and is higher in men. Family history is an independent risk factor in all phases. Genotype C carries higher HCC risk than genotype B in Asia — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- Australian HCC surveillance guidelines (MJA 2025): for CHB without cirrhosis in people of **Asian or Pacific background, consider 6-monthly surveillance for males aged ≥40** (females ≥50) using ultrasound, with or without AFP (Rec 5.1, weak). With a family history of HCC in a first-degree relative, offer it from 40, possibly starting 10 years before the earliest family case. F3 fibrosis warrants individual consideration, and progression should be monitored by elastography. Transient elastography and FIB-4 are not yet on the MBS — [MJA 2025;223(8)](https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2025/223/8/clinical-practice-guidelines-hepatocellular-carcinoma-surveillance-people-high)
- WHO 2024: patient-facing summary lists liver imaging and bloods at least every 6-12 months, and ALT plus HBV DNA every 12 months. Treatment thresholds: HBV DNA >2,000 IU/mL with ALT >ULN (30 U/L men), or persistently abnormal ALT alone, or significant fibrosis — [Hepatitis B Foundation simplified WHO 2024 guide (PDF)](https://www.hepb.org/assets/WHO-Treatment-Guidelines-Final-Simplified-.pdf); [WHO 2024 publication page](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240091368)
- EASL 2025: test anti-HAV IgG and offer hepatitis A vaccination if negative. Test for HCV, HDV and HIV coinfection, and consider HEV if transaminases rise without explanation — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
Suggested questions for the doctor, from the cited guidelines:
1. "Which phase am I in (HBeAg status, last HBV DNA level)? Am I on an antiviral?"
2. "Does my twice-yearly bloodwork include HBV DNA and quantitative HBsAg, not just liver function tests?" ("All normal" may refer to LFTs only.)
3. "Was my ALT under 30 U/L?" (the stricter male cut-off).
4. "When was my last fibrosis assessment (FibroScan or FIB-4), and is one due, perhaps as a post-weight-loss baseline with steatosis (CAP)?"
5. "When should I start 6-monthly ultrasound ± AFP? Do I have a family history of liver cancer?"
6. "Have I been tested for hepatitis D, hepatitis C and HIV, and am I immune to hepatitis A?"
7. "Is my HBV genotype known (B vs C)?"

### Gaps
- AASLD 2018 and APASL texts were not fetched; EASL 2025 and Australian guidance were used as the primary references. The AASLD 2018 update is now 8 years old, and an AASLD update status was not checked.
- The Australian ASHM/GESA HBV management interval recommendations were not fetched directly.

## 12. Transmission: household, sexual contacts, travel

### Takeaway
HBV spreads through blood and sexual fluids, not casual contact, food or kissing. The key steps are vaccinating non-immune household and sexual contacts, not sharing razors, toothbrushes or nail clippers, covering cuts, and barrier protection with any unvaccinated partner. EASL 2025 and Australian guidance both call for screening and vaccinating household and sexual contacts.

### Cited Findings
- EASL 2025: household members, sexual partners and close contacts of people with HBV should be screened for HBV. Antiviral therapy also reduces transmission. Transmission in healthcare work has almost always occurred at HBV DNA >20,000 IU/mL and is considered unlikely below 2,000 IU/mL — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- Australian Immunisation Handbook: susceptible household and close contacts of people with hepatitis B are recommended to receive hepatitis B vaccine — [Immunisation Handbook](https://immunisationhandbook.health.gov.au/recommendations/household-or-other-close-contacts-of-people-with-hepatitis-b-are-recommended-to-receive-hepatitis-b-vaccine-if-they-are-not-immune)
- Practical precautions: do not share razors, toothbrushes or other objects that may carry blood; susceptible household members, particularly sexual partners, should be vaccinated — [South Dakota DOH fact sheet](https://doh.sd.gov/media/fo1bov10/hepatitis_b_diseases_fact_sheet.pdf). A hepatology guideline table says people who are HBsAg-positive can share food and utensils and kiss, and should use barrier protection if a partner is unvaccinated — [PMC5975958, Table 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5975958/table/T5)
- HBV can survive at least 7 days on surfaces — [NYC DOHMH](https://a860-gpp.nyc.gov/downloads/xk81jm35n?locale=en)

### Inferences
- Family meals in Taiwan (shared dishes, shared chopsticks) are not a transmission route according to the sources above. Blood-contact items are the concern.
- Gym relevance: cover any cuts or blisters (e.g. from barbell knurling). No source specifically addresses gyms; this is inference from blood-borne transmission.

### Gaps
- The current CDC page wording was not fetched directly; secondary sources citing CDC/ACIP were used.

## 13. Vaccines relevant to an adult with chronic HBV (Australian context)

### Takeaway
Australian guidance recommends **hepatitis A vaccine** for non-immune people with chronic liver disease of any cause, including chronic hepatitis B (2 doses at least 6 months apart). **Chronic liver disease, including chronic hepatitis, is listed as a pneumococcal risk condition** (13vPCV, then 23vPPV at 12 months and again 5 or more years later). Annual influenza vaccination is universally recommended. COVID-19 booster eligibility for his group was not verified in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Hepatitis A: the Handbook recommends hepatitis A vaccine for people with chronic liver disease of any aetiology who are not immune, including chronic hepatitis B. Two doses, minimum 6 months apart. Vaccinate early, since response falls in advanced liver disease — [Immunisation Handbook: chronic liver disease and hepatitis A](https://immunisationhandbook.health.gov.au/node/577); [Hepatitis A chapter](https://immunisationhandbook.health.gov.au/node/146)
- EASL 2025: test anti-HAV IgG; if negative, hepatitis A vaccination may be offered, especially in advanced chronic liver disease — [EASL 2025 PDF](https://www.hepb.org/assets/Uploads/EASL-guidelines-May-2025.pdf)
- Pneumococcal: the Handbook lists chronic liver disease among risk conditions. The adult schedule is 1 dose 13vPCV at diagnosis, 23vPPV 12 months later, and a second 23vPPV at least 5 years after that. 15vPCV and 20vPCV are alternatives, not NIP-funded — [Handbook pneumococcal infographic, Aug 2024 (PDF)](https://immunisationhandbook.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/Infographic%20-%20Pneumococcal%20vaccination%20for%20people%20with%20risk%20conditions%20for%20pneumococcal%20disease_Aug24.pdf). The 2020 NIP decision tree defines the category as "chronic liver disease, including chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis and biliary atresia" — [NIP decision tree 2020 (PDF)](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2020/06/national-immunisation-program-pneumococcal-vaccination-schedule-from-1-july-2020-clinical-decision-tree-for-vaccination-providers.pdf). Victoria lists chronic liver disease among conditions that qualify adults for free pneumococcal vaccine — [Better Health Channel](https://www.betterhealth.vic.gov.au/health/HealthyLiving/pneumococcal-disease-immunisation)
- Sleep and vaccine response: men with objectively short sleep had substantially weaker antibody responses (section 3) — [Spiegel 2023](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-associations-between-insufficient-sleep-du/)

### Inferences
- Before the Jan-Feb 2027 trip: ask the GP about (a) hepatitis A immunity (blood test) and vaccination if negative, (b) whether pneumococcal vaccination applies to him (whether a well-controlled, non-cirrhotic HBV carrier counts as "chronic liver disease" here is a clinical judgement), (c) the 2026 influenza vaccine (Southern Hemisphere season, if not already done; whether a further dose before Taiwan's winter flu season is advisable is a question for the GP), and (d) COVID-19 booster eligibility. Sleep 7 h or more around each vaccination.
- Whether WA funds pneumococcal vaccination for adults with chronic liver disease under the NIP was not confirmed. Ask the GP.

### Gaps
- Influenza: the Handbook's universal annual recommendation for everyone aged ≥6 months is well known but was **not re-verified** in this session.
- COVID-19: current ATAGI 2025-2026 recommendations for adults 18-64 with a chronic condition were **not verified**.
- Timing advice for flu vaccination for Southern-to-Northern-Hemisphere winter travel was not researched.

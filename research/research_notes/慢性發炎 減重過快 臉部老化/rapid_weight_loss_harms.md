The subject: man, 36, 177 cm, 102 → ~88 kg in ~14.5 weeks (≈1 kg/wk, ≈1 %/wk), estimated ~22 % BF (FFM ≈68 kg), resistance training 5×/wk, protein ~1.9 g/kg, chronic HBV (controlled), new dry or peeling skin and itch.

## 1. Definitions: what is "too fast" (kg/week, %/week, relative to body fat)

### Takeaway
Clinical obesity guidance still says **0.5–1 kg/week**. Sports-science guidance for resistance-trained people says **0.5–1.0 % of body weight per week, at the low end (≈0.5 %/wk) as you get leaner**. Leaner people lose a larger share of lean tissue per kg lost (the Forbes/Hall relationship), so the "safe" rate shrinks as body fat falls. At ~22 % BF and dropping toward 15–18 %, this man's current ~1 %/wk sits at the **top** of the athletic range. It is fine as a ceiling but not as a target for the final phase.

### Cited Findings
- **NICE NG246 (Overweight and obesity management, published 14 Jan 2025, updated 8 Jan 2026)**: secondary summaries state that NICE expects weight-management programmes to produce **no more than 0.5–1 kg (1–2 lb) per week**. I reached only the NICE landing page and could not read the exact wording myself. — [GPnotebook summary of NG246](https://gpnotebook.com/en-GB/pages/obesity/management-of-obesity/conservative-management-of-obesity); [NICE NG246 page](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng246/chapter/Finding-more-information-and-committee-details)
- Earlier NICE guidance carried the same figure: "a maximum weekly weight loss of 0.5–1 kg", with a 5–10 % total target. — [Cambridge Core paper describing NICE guidance](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/product/09156FD25ED1B788CFE10E7C96A3BB05/core-reader)
- **Helms et al. (2020, J Hum Kinet, physique athletes)**: slower rates (**≤0.5 % body mass/week**) are preferable to limit fat-free-mass (FFM) loss during contest prep. Protein 1.8–2.7 g/kg. — [search summary of Helms/Roberts 2020 review, via Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1687463)
- **Ruiz-Castellano et al. 2021, *Nutrients* 13:3255 (narrative review, resistance-trained athletes)**: target **0.5–1.0 %/week**, low end advised. "Lower body fat calls for a more conservative deficit." Case studies showed more FFM retained at 0.5 %/wk than at 0.7 % or 1 %/wk. In the 1 %/wk case, FFM was **42.8 % of total weight lost**. The authors also say the literature in resistance-trained athletes is "very limited". — [PMC8471721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8471721/)
- **Garthe et al. 2011 (IJSNEM, elite athletes)**: compared ~0.7 %/wk vs ~1.4 %/wk loss with resistance training. Conclusion: "a moderate energy restriction combined with resistance training leading to a weekly weight loss of 0.7 % seems preferable." — [record of the trial](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4017680?lng=en) **[landmark, pre-2023]**. The lean-mass numbers often quoted (slow group LBM about +2.1 % vs fast about −0.2 %, and more fat lost in the slow group) are **[UNVERIFIED]**: my searches did not reach the primary results table.
- **Forbes/Hall relationship [landmark]**: Forbes fitted L = 10.4·ln(F) + 14.2 (kg) from cross-sectional data. The share of weight change that is lean tissue depends on initial body fat, so **leaner people lose a larger share of lean mass**. Hall (2007) extended it to large weight changes through the P-ratio, which depends on initial body composition. — [Hall 2007, Br J Nutr, "Forbes's theory revisited" (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17367567/); [Hall, "Dynamics of human body weight change" (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0802.3234)
- Caveat: Eric Helms has argued that the Forbes/Hall models are not usable for resistance-trained people, because training and protein change partitioning. — [as summarised in search results; Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=51931)
- Low energy availability (EA) thresholds for men: EA **<25 kcal/kg FFM/day** is linked to greater FFM loss and to hormonal and psychological problems (Fagerberg). For women, hormonal changes appear below about 30 kcal/kg FFM/day. — [Ruiz-Castellano 2021, PMC8471721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8471721/)

### Inferences
- **Forbes curve applied to this person (untrained model, an upper-bound estimate)**: dL/dF = 10.4/F. At fat mass ≈20 kg (88 kg × ~22 %), dL/dF ≈ 0.52, so lean ≈ 0.52/1.52 ≈ **34 % of each kg lost**. At ~12 kg fat (≈81 kg at 15 %), dL/dF ≈ 0.87, so lean ≈ **46 %**. Resistance training plus ~1.9 g/kg protein should beat these untrained predictions by a wide margin, but the direction holds: the same %/week costs proportionally more lean tissue as he approaches 15 %.
- His FFM is ≈68 kg. An EA floor of 25 kcal/kg FFM is ≈1,700 kcal/day *after* subtracting exercise energy. His ≥1,700 kcal hard limit is a total-intake limit. With daily treadmill plus 5× lifting (maybe 400–700 kcal/day of exercise), 1,700 kcal total would put EA at roughly 15–19 kcal/kg FFM, **below the male LEA threshold**. At the current 2,520 kcal he is likely around 27–31 kcal/kg FFM (depending on exercise expenditure), which is acceptable. **The 1,700 kcal floor is too low for his training volume and should be an EA-based floor, not a total-intake floor.**
- His rule of "max 1 %/wk on the 14-day trend" matches the top of the evidence range. A tighter rule supported by the evidence: **≤0.7 %/wk (~0.6 kg/wk) now, ≤0.5 %/wk once under ~84 kg / ~18 % BF.**

### Gaps
- I could not open the primary text of NICE NG246, NHS, NIH ("1–2 lb/week"), ADA/Obesity Canada or Australian NHMRC (2013 guidelines) to confirm their exact rate wording. Treat the 0.5–1 kg/wk and 1–2 lb/wk figures as consistent across bodies but verified only for NICE, and only via secondary sources.
- I found no 2023–2026 RCT in trained men that directly compares %/week rates. The rate evidence in lean trained people is still Garthe 2011 plus case studies.
- No ISSN position stand specific to rate of loss was verified in this session.

## 2. Fast vs slow: lean mass, fat loss, RMR, regain (incl. 2023+ evidence)

### Takeaway
At equal total weight loss, **gradual loss removes about 1 kg more fat and preserves RMR better**. FFM differences trend toward gradual but are not significant in overweight or obese samples. **Regain is not affected by rate** (Purcell 2014). I found **no 2023–2026 meta-analysis** that updates this question.

### Cited Findings
- **Ashtary-Larky et al. 2020, Br J Nutr 124(11)** (7 studies, 6 RCTs; gradual arms 9–36 wk vs rapid 5–12 wk; total loss matched within 2 kg). Gradual vs rapid: body weight WMD 0.03 kg (ns); **fat mass −1.00 kg (95 % CI −1.70 to −0.29)**; **body fat % −0.83 (−1.49 to −0.17)**; FFM +0.74 kg (−0.15 to 1.64, ns); **RMR higher in gradual** (reported as 407.48 kJ, but the CI is 78.78–118.01 kJ, so the point estimate is probably a typo, around 97 kJ ≈ 23 kcal/day). The paper defines no kg/week cutoff. — [Cambridge Core, BJN 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/effects-of-gradual-weight-loss-v-rapid-weight-loss-on-body-composition-and-rmr-a-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/427E2A512D278FC053CEBB73995FEEFC)
- An earlier Ashtary-Larky 2017 review (IJEM) reported that rapid loss came at the expense of muscle mass. — [Brieflands IJEM](https://brieflands.com/journals/ijem/articles/13249)
- **Purcell et al. 2014, Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2(12):954–62 [landmark]**: n = 204, BMI 30–45. 12-wk rapid (VLCD) vs 36-wk gradual loss, then 144-wk maintenance. Regain was **71.2 % (gradual) vs 70.5 % (rapid)**, and 76.3 % in both groups by ITT. Rate did not affect regain. More of the rapid group reached the ≥12.5 % threshold (81 % vs 50 %). — [Monash research portal](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-rate-of-weight-loss-on-long-term-weight-management-/); [Dr Sharma commentary](https://www.drsharma.ca/does-the-rate-of-weight-loss-predict-your-rate-of-weight-regain)
- A Norwegian RCT (NCT03776552, ≥286 participants, 16-wk rapid vs gradual, 1-yr follow-up) is registered. A trade-press summary says it found rapid loss "more efficient and sustainable", but I did not verify the primary publication. — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03776552); [NutritionInsight news](https://www.nutritioninsight.com/news/rapid-weight-loss-sustainable-trial-results.html)
- **Diet breaks (MATADOR, Byrne 2018, men with obesity, no resistance training)**: 2 wk on / 2 wk maintenance for 30 wk vs 16 wk continuous dieting. The intermittent group lost more fat with similar FFM loss, and its **adjusted RMR fall was −86 vs −179 kcal/day**. **Peos et al.** (resistance-trained athletes): intermittent and continuous dieting gave similar fat loss and FFM retention. The minimum effective break length is unknown. — [Ruiz-Castellano 2021, PMC8471721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8471721/)
- **Refeeds**: 3 days of carbohydrate at about 40 % above maintenance raised **leptin 28 %** and energy expenditure 7 %, and both returned to baseline once the deficit resumed (Dirlewanger). — [PMC8471721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8471721/)

### Inferences
- In people with obesity, rate mostly matters for fat-vs-lean partitioning and adaptation, not for regain. In a man nearing 15–20 % BF, the partitioning penalty grows (section 1), so the case for slowing down is stronger for him than the obesity meta-analyses suggest.
- His planned maintenance block from 2026-12-14 (week ~22) is later than his own "diet break after 10–12 weeks" rule. In trained people, evidence for diet breaks is neutral for body composition and positive for adherence and RMR in sedentary men with obesity. Neither result makes a break mandatory, but both support it.

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 meta-analysis on rate vs body composition was found. The ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03776552 results paper was not verified.

## 3. Lean mass and muscle: GLP-1 era data, mitigation

### Takeaway
In drug trials without structured training, **~25–40 % of weight lost is DXA "lean mass"**: STEP-1 semaglutide ≈39–40 %, SURMOUNT-1 tirzepatide ≈25–26 %. Conte, Hall and Klein (JAMA 2024) argue that the *relative* lean loss is smaller than the relative fat loss, and that most adults will not become sarcopenic. Critics note that DXA "lean" is not muscle, and that skeletal muscle has rarely been measured directly. Protein plus resistance training attenuates the loss.

### Cited Findings
- **STEP-1 DXA substudy (Wilding et al. 2021)**: n = 140 (95 semaglutide, 45 placebo). Weight −15.0 % vs −3.6 %. **Total lean mass −9.7 % from baseline**, but lean mass as a proportion of body mass *rose* 3.0 percentage points. The "~39 % of weight lost was lean" figure is a derived ratio from secondary sources. One 2026 analysis gives **38.6 % (treatment-policy) / 40.0 % (trial-product)**. — [STEP-1 substudy, PMC8089287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/8089287); [derived-ratio summary (secondary)](https://therxindex.com/research/glp1-lean-mass-by-drug/)
- **SURMOUNT-1 DXA substudy (tirzepatide)**: n ≈ 160, weight −21.3 % at 72 wk, fat mass −33.9 %, lean mass −10.9%. About **74 % of loss was fat and 26 % lean**. These figures come from secondary summaries; I did not open the primary NEJM 2022 supplement. — [secondary summary](https://acibademinternational.com/blog/muscle-loss-on-glp-1-medicines-how-much-is-lean-mass-and-how-to-protect-it/); [Barbell Medicine summary](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/glp-1-muscle-loss/)
- **SEMALEAN (prospective, semaglutide 2.4 mg, n = 106 completers, DXA)**: fat mass −14 % at month 7 and −18 % at month 12. Lean mass fell (−3 kg at month 7) and then **stabilised**. — [PMC12673431](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12673431/)
- **Conte C, Hall KD, Klein S. JAMA 2024;332(1):9–10**: "Is weight loss-induced muscle mass loss clinically relevant?" Relative FFM/SMM loss is smaller than relative fat loss, and "most adults who lose weight ... should not experience sarcopenia". Protein and exercise attenuate FFM/SMM loss. A letter in reply objects that SMM was not directly measured in GLP-1 trials and that the Viewpoint assumes SMM is a constant 50 % of FFM. — [PubMed-indexed record](https://nufind.nu.edu.sa/EdsRecord/cmedm,38829659); [Healio coverage](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20241226/sarcopenia-unlikely-for-most-after-weight-loss-but-more-data-needed-on-older-adults); [PracticeUpdate](https://practiceupdate.com/content/is-weight-loss-induced-muscle-mass-loss-clinically-relevant/166779)
- DXA cannot separate contractile muscle from water, organ tissue or intramuscular fat. Small MRI studies of tirzepatide suggest muscle volume falls less than DXA lean implies (mostly conference data). — [secondary summary](https://therxindex.com/research/glp1-lean-mass-by-drug/)
- 2025 narrative review (incretin era): incretin drugs cause "rapid reductions in muscle and bone mass". Nutrition (protein) and physical activity (resistance training) are the mitigation tools. — [PMC12787956](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12787956/); see also [PMC11723338 (etiology of reduced muscle mass with surgical/pharmacological weight loss)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11723338/)
- **Protein during a deficit (trained)**: ranges cited are 1.6–2.4 g/kg (Hector & Phillips; higher with bigger deficits and lower BF), 2.2–3.0 g/kg (Ruiz-Castellano), 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM (Helms), with no benefit seen above about 2.6 g/kg (ISSN). — [PMC8471721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8471721/)

### Inferences
- GLP-1 trial participants were mostly untrained adults with obesity. A 36-year-old lifting 5×/week on ~1.9 g/kg protein should land well below 25–40 % lean loss. His own 14 kg loss would need a body-composition scan to confirm this, and InBody is no longer available.
- His protein floor of **≥1.6 g/kg is the bottom of the range**. Evidence for lean people in a deficit favours **≥2.0–2.2 g/kg body weight (≈2.3–2.6 g/kg FFM)** during the final leaner phase. The current ~1.9 g/kg is adequate but not generous. No supplements are needed; this is a food-based target.

### Gaps
- I did not open the primary SURMOUNT-1 body-composition paper. The 25–26 % figure is secondary but consistent across sources.
- No 2023+ trial of trained men measured skeletal muscle by MRI across different loss rates.

## 4. Gallstones

### Takeaway
Rapid loss raises the risk of gallstones (cholesterol supersaturation of bile, plus gallbladder stasis when fat intake is very low). The "**>1.5 kg/week**" threshold is widely repeated but I could **not** verify it from a primary source. Adequate **dietary fat (≈19–30 % of energy) is protective** compared with near-fat-free VLCDs. GLP-1 RAs add risk over placebo.

### Cited Findings
- **Stokes et al. 2014, Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol [landmark meta-analysis]**: 13 RCTs, n = 1,836. "UDCA and/or higher dietary fat content appear to prevent formation of gallstones" during weight loss. UDCA RR 0.33 (0.18–0.60), NNT 9. Rapid weight loss increases stone formation. A secondary summary reports high-fat vs low-fat diet **RR ≈0.09**, and diets with 19–30 % fat producing fewer stones than diets with 3–5 % fat. — [PubMed 24321208](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24321208/); [Dr Sharma summary](https://www.drsharma.ca/how-to-prevent-gallstones-during-weight-loss)
- **Festi et al. 1998, Int J Obes [landmark]**: on a VLCD, gallstones developed in **6/11 (54.5 %) on the lower-fat diet vs 0/11 on the higher-fat diet**. — [Springer, IJO](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/sj.ijo.0800634)
- Historic VLCD figures of **30–70 %** gallstone incidence, with risk rising as loss gets faster, appear only in secondary sources. — [Substack summary (secondary, low quality)](https://lauralisowski.substack.com/p/glp-1-drugs-and-gallbladder-risk)
- **GLP-1 RAs**: He et al. 2022, JAMA Intern Med (76 RCTs) is reported as RR ≈1.37 for gallbladder or biliary disease, higher at higher doses, with longer use and when used for weight loss. Secondary sources disagree on the figures, so they are **[UNVERIFIED]** against the primary paper. A 2025 meta-analysis of GLP-1 RAs in non-diabetic obesity is also available. — [PMC12670982](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12670982/); [Substack summary (secondary)](https://lauralisowski.substack.com/p/glp-1-drugs-and-gallbladder-risk)

### Inferences
- At ~1 kg/wk with fat at ≥0.6 g/kg (~53 g/day ≈ 19–20 % of 2,520 kcal), he sits at the bottom edge of the protective 19–30 % band. **Keeping fat at ≥0.6–0.8 g/kg and spreading it across meals** (gallbladder emptying needs a fat stimulus) is supported. His fat floor is consistent with the evidence.
- Warning signs: right-upper-quadrant or epigastric pain after fatty meals, lasting more than 30 minutes, possibly radiating to the right shoulder, with fever or jaundice. These are standard clinical red flags; I did not fetch a specific source for them.

### Gaps
- The exact rate threshold (">1.5 kg/wk") could not be verified from a primary source. The He 2022 JAMA IM figures were not opened.

## 5. Bone

### Takeaway
Weight loss lowers hip and spine BMD roughly in proportion to how much is lost. In a 2024 semaglutide RCT, hip BMD fell about 2.6 % and spine about 2.1 % vs placebo. In a 2024 Danish RCT, **adding exercise to GLP-1 therapy prevented the BMD loss**. Resistance plus impact training is the main protection, and this man does both.

### Cited Findings
- **Semaglutide RCT, eClinicalMedicine 2024** (adults at increased fracture risk, 52 wk): lower total hip (about −0.020 g/cm²) and lumbar spine (about −0.018) BMD vs placebo. Secondary sources give **−2.6 % hip and −2.1 % spine** vs placebo. No femoral neck difference. — [PMC11087719](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/11087719/)
- **Exercise + GLP-1 RA (Danish RCT, 2024, liraglutide not semaglutide)**: four 30-minute supervised sessions per week. **No BMD change at hip or spine in exercise + drug vs placebo**, despite greater weight loss (16.88 kg vs 13.74 kg for drug alone). I saw only secondary coverage, not the primary journal (reported as JAMA Netw Open 2024 **[UNVERIFIED]**). — [MDedge coverage](https://www.mdedge9-ma1.mdedge.com/endocrinology/article/269813/obesity/exercise-plus-glp-1-ras-upped-weight-loss-bone-retention)
- A large CV outcomes trial (>17,000) reportedly found about a **5-fold higher hip and pelvic fracture incidence in adults ≥75 on semaglutide**. This is secondary, **[UNVERIFIED]**. — [Oregon State blog (secondary)](https://blogs.oregonstate.edu/wander/?p=3139)

### Inferences
- Bone risk is driven by the magnitude of weight loss and is offset by loading. For a 36-year-old lifting 5×/week it is a low-priority concern. It would matter more if training dropped or intake fell to LEA levels (bone turnover markers fall at EA <30 kcal/kg FFM in female athletes; male data are sparse).

### Gaps
- No primary 2023+ data on BMD change by *rate* of loss in young trained men were found.

## 6. Hair (telogen effluvium)

### Takeaway
Rapid weight loss is an established trigger of **telogen effluvium (TE)**, with shedding typically **2–4 months after the trigger** and recovery over 3–6 (to 12) months. The 2025–2026 GLP-1 cohort data link TE most strongly to tirzepatide, the drug producing the largest and fastest loss, which supports weight-loss stress, not the drug, as the driver.

### Cited Findings
- **Gupta et al. 2026, *Science Progress*, systematic review**: 24 of 133 studies included. **Tirzepatide, with the greatest weight loss, was most often linked to TE**, and rapid weight loss was flagged as a contributor. — [PMC13100445](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13100445/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00368504261444578)
- **Propensity-matched TriNetX cohort (2025/26)**: semaglutide and tirzepatide were associated with higher odds of nonscarring hair loss, especially TE. Tirzepatide was linked at all follow-up points, and "follow-up BMI findings suggest weight-loss-associated physiologic stress may contribute." — [PubMed 42647098](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42647098/)
- **EADV 2025 TriNetX analysis**: GLP-1 RA use was associated with TE, androgenetic alopecia and nonscarring hair loss, but not alopecia areata. Presenters raised possible *unmasking* of underlying hair loss. — [Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/telogen-effluvium-and-androgenic-alopecia-rates-high-among-2025a1000oo9)
- A 2025 scoping review found most studies **lacked dermatological confirmation** of TE. — [PMC12431796](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12431796/)
- Proposed mechanism: rapid loss leading to deficits of iron, zinc, vitamin D, biotin and protein. — [PMC11909624](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11909624/)
- Timing and prognosis: TE "often improves in 3–6 months" (Cleveland Clinic), or 6–12 months once weight stabilises (lower-quality patient site). — [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/does-ozempic-cause-hair-loss); [CNN 2025](https://www.cnn.com/2025/11/14/health/glp-1-hair-loss-telogen-effluvium-wellness)

### Inferences
- The cut began on 2026-07-15, so the TE window from the early fast phase is roughly **mid-Sept to mid-Nov 2026, i.e. now**. Increased shedding in the shower or on the pillow over the next 4–8 weeks would fit TE and would be expected to resolve. It is not a reason to stop, but it is a signal to stop accelerating.
- Testing ferritin, zinc and vitamin D in the next blood test is supported by the mechanism literature. Supplementing is outside this person's rules: test, then a doctor decides.

### Gaps
- No dose-response data between kg/week and TE incidence were found. The "2–4 months after trigger" timing is standard dermatology teaching; I did not fetch a primary source for it **[UNVERIFIED citation]**.

## 7. Hormones (testosterone, T3, leptin, cortisol) in men

### Takeaway
In drug-free male physique athletes dieting to stage leanness (~5 % BF), **testosterone falls from 543–922 to 175–227 ng/dL (hypogonadal range)**, cortisol rises and T3 and leptin fall. Recovery after re-feeding takes roughly **1–3 months**. These extremes come with very low body fat and long preps. At 15–18 % BF the expected suppression is far smaller, but it scales with deficit depth and leanness.

### Cited Findings
- **Schoenfeld, Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2023, J Funct Morphol Kinesiol 8(2):59** (systematic review of 11 case studies, 15 athletes). Men (n = 6): testosterone at baseline **543–922 ng/dL fell to 175–227 ng/dL** by contest. Cortisol rose in all 3 athletes measured. Leptin was stable to slightly lower in men. Mean weight-loss rate was **−0.42 kg/wk in men**, with BF going from 14.5 % to 5.8 %. Resting HR fell 1–26 bpm in 9 of 10 athletes. — [PMC10204542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10204542/)
- **Rossow et al. 2013 [landmark case]**: testosterone went from 9.22 to 2.27 ng/mL during prep. Leptin fell 47 % and T3 45 % (insulin −83 %). BF went from 14.8 % to 4.5 %. Testosterone returned to baseline during recovery, and BF returned to 14.6 %. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256501356_Natural_Bodybuilding_Competition_Preparation_and_Recovery_A_12-Month_Case_Study)
- **Post-competition recovery review (JISSN 2026)**: describes reduced testosterone and T3 and elevated cortisol in prep. Summaries say values recover to within normal ranges or baseline within **1–3 months** of increased energy intake and regained body mass. The review notes that heterogeneity limits interpretation. — [JISSN 2026, doi 10.1080/15502783.2026.2676190](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2026.2676190)
- **2024 case report (drug-free bodybuilder, PMC11601077)**: documents low-energy-availability indicators during prep and peak week. — [PMC11601077](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11601077/)
- Leptin in a male bodybuilder cohort fell from 9.6 % to 8.0 % BF but did not fall further at 6.5 %, which suggests a floor. — [PMC10204542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10204542/)
- Low-fat diets (≤20 % fat) are associated with lower testosterone (confounded). A slower rate (~0.5 %/wk) and adequate fat may help. — [PMC8471721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8471721/)

### Inferences
- At ~22 % BF, ≥0.6 g/kg fat and a moderate deficit, clinically meaningful hypogonadism is unlikely. **Libido loss, morning-erection loss, cold intolerance or lethargy** would be the practical tells. A serum total testosterone and free T3 measured during the next bloodwork would anchor this.

### Gaps
- No 2023+ controlled study of hormone change versus *rate* of loss in non-competitor men at 15–25 % BF was found.

## 8. Metabolic adaptation and regain vs rate

### Takeaway
RMR falls more with rapid loss (Ashtary-Larky 2020), but **regain over ~3 years is the same regardless of rate** (Purcell 2014). Intermittent dieting reduced the RMR drop in sedentary men with obesity (MATADOR) but not in trained athletes (Peos).

### Cited Findings
- Gradual loss preserved RMR better than rapid loss (about 97 kJ ≈ 23 kcal/day; see the CI note in section 2). — [BJN 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/effects-of-gradual-weight-loss-v-rapid-weight-loss-on-body-composition-and-rmr-a-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/427E2A512D278FC053CEBB73995FEEFC)
- Regain was 70.5 % (rapid) vs 71.2 % (gradual) at 144 wk. — [Monash](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-rate-of-weight-loss-on-long-term-weight-management-/)
- MATADOR adjusted RMR: −86 (intermittent) vs −179 (continuous) kcal/day. Peos (trained): no body-composition difference. Hall's dynamic model: a 500 kcal/day deficit plateaus at about 12 months, and adaptation comes through lower NEAT, efficiency, hormones and appetite. — [PMC8471721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8471721/)
- Weight regain after stopping GLP-1 RAs follows a nonlinear trajectory (medRxiv 2025 preprint, not peer-reviewed). — [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.09.25328726.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- The RMR penalty of rapid loss is real but small. The bigger regain risk for him is behavioural: hunger and food preoccupation (section 9) and the transition out of the deficit. The planned maintenance block is the evidence-aligned answer.

### Gaps
- No 2023+ head-to-head trial of rate vs adaptive thermogenesis in trained men was found.

## 9. Mood, sleep, cognition, binge-eating risk

### Takeaway
In contest-prep case data, **mood disturbance, fatigue and poor subjective sleep** rise in the final ~2 months. **Cognitive restraint rises ~40 %** and uncontrolled eating can rise in men. These effects scale with deficit depth and leanness.

### Cited Findings
- Total mood disturbance rose in two case reports in the 2 months before competition, with lower energy and higher fatigue. Anxiety rose occasionally. — [PMC10204542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10204542/)
- Cognitive restraint rose about 40 % during the competition phase. Uncontrolled eating **rose in the male athlete**. — [PMC10204542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10204542/)
- Sleep: Pardue 2017 reported bouts of insomnia. Petrizzo 2017 found actigraphy unchanged but **PSQI showing poor sleep**, so subjective sleep worsened before objective sleep did. — [PMC10204542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10204542/)
- Men with EA <25 kcal/kg FFM: psychological problems are reported alongside FFM loss. — [PMC8471721](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8471721/)

### Inferences
- Garmin sleep score and HRV may lag subjective sleep complaints, so /feel notes ("hungry all the time", "thinking about food", "irritable", "waking at night") are a valid early signal.

### Gaps
- The Minnesota Starvation Experiment (Keys 1950: food preoccupation, binge eating on refeeding) is the classic landmark source but was not fetched this session **[UNVERIFIED]**. No 2023+ RCT on binge-eating risk vs rate of loss was found.

## 10. Skin: xerosis, itch, wound healing, loose skin

### Takeaway
Evidence directly linking caloric restriction rate to dry skin is **sparse**. The documented nutritional causes of xerosis are **essential fatty acid deficiency** (higher transepidermal water loss), **zinc**, niacin, vitamin C and B12 deficits, and low total energy and fat intake. These are plausible but unproven for this man.

### Cited Findings
- EFA deficiency increases epidermal permeability and transepidermal water loss. In children with dry, scaly leg skin, low energy and low fat intake were common and EFA deficiency was suspected. — [Karger, Ann Nutr Metab](https://karger.com/anm/article-pdf/39/1/9/2221698/000177837.pdf)
- Xerosis appears in reference tables of cutaneous signs of nutritional deficiency (zinc, EFA, niacin, B12, vitamin C). Patients often have more than one deficiency, and restrictive diets are a risk factor. — [AMBOSS](https://www.amboss.com/us/knowledge/Cutaneous_manifestations_of_nutritional_deficiencies); [MDedge, skin findings of nutritional deficiencies](https://www.mdedge.com/content/skin-findings-associated-nutritional-deficiencies/page/0/1)

### Inferences
- With fat ≥0.6 g/kg (~53 g/day) from mixed foods, frank EFA deficiency is unlikely. Low fat *quality* (few oily fish, nuts or seeds) or low zinc from a narrower diet is plausible. Non-diet causes are at least as likely: frequent showers after daily treadmill and lifting, hot water, soap and season. Dry skin plus itch can also come from hepatobiliary causes (cholestatic pruritus) or hypothyroidism. **With hepatitis B, new generalised itch is worth mentioning to his hepatologist at the next visit** rather than being assumed to be dietary.
- Loose skin after a large loss: 14 kg from 102 kg is moderate, and no 2023+ data were found that quantify it by rate.

### Gaps
- No study was found that quantifies xerosis incidence versus rate of weight loss. No primary data on wound healing at moderate deficits.

## 12. Liver: transient ALT rise; relevance to hepatitis B

### Takeaway
Rapid caloric restriction commonly causes an **early, transient ALT rise (weeks 2–6)** that normalises by about 16–24 weeks, and the liver improves overall. Older surgical and VLCD literature describes worsening steatohepatitis with very rapid loss. **No data were found on rate of loss in chronic HBV.** For him, an ALT rise during a cut is ambiguous (diet artefact vs HBV flare) and needs HBV DNA to interpret.

### Cited Findings
- **Nutrition & Metabolism 2020 (n = 105, severe obesity, 24-wk milk-based meal replacement)**: ALT **rose at 2 weeks**, then declined below baseline by 24 weeks. The largest falls were in people who started with high ALT. The authors propose that a fast, large restriction briefly worsens steatosis before improvement. — [PMC7565317](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7565317/)
- **Anderson (Obesity Society meeting report)**: ALT rose within **3–6 weeks** in nearly all rapid-loss patients and **normalised in 98 % by 16 weeks**. No biopsy data. — [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/liver-enzyme-spike-after-weight-loss-deemed-transient)

### Inferences
- He is past week 14 at a moderate rate, so a diet-driven ALT rise should already have peaked and resolved. If his next ALT is high, this literature does **not** justify assuming "diet artefact". With HBV it should be interpreted alongside HBV DNA by his doctor. Weight and fat loss generally benefit livers with concurrent steatosis.

### Gaps
- No 2023+ studies on weight-loss rate in chronic HBV were found. The classic finding that very rapid loss worsens portal inflammation and fibrosis (Andersen 1991, J Hepatol) is **[UNVERIFIED]** this session.

## 13. Warning signs a person can monitor: which have evidence

### Takeaway
The best-supported self-monitorable signals are: **rate of loss itself (trend %/wk)**, **strength or performance falling on main lifts**, **energy, mood and sleep quality (subjective first)**, **libido and morning erections** (testosterone), **cold intolerance** (T3), **hair shedding at 2–4 months**, and **hunger and food preoccupation**. Resting HR tends to *fall* during prep, so it is a poor "too fast" alarm on its own. HRV evidence was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Absolute strength and power decline during prep, while relative strength is often maintained. **Absolute power fell in all athletes assessed.** — [PMC10204542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10204542/)
- **Resting HR fell 1–26 bpm in 9 of 10** prepping athletes, and BP fell too. Lower RHR during a deep deficit reflects reduced sympathetic tone and T3, not necessarily fitness. — [PMC10204542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10204542/)
- Mood disturbance, fatigue, poor PSQI and insomnia bouts appear late in prep. — [PMC10204542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10204542/)
- Testosterone drops into the hypogonadal range at stage leanness, and T3 and leptin fall. — [PMC10204542](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10204542/); [Rossow 2013](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256501356_Natural_Bodybuilding_Competition_Preparation_and_Recovery_A_12-Month_Case_Study)
- Hair shedding (TE) is linked to the magnitude and speed of loss. — [PMC13100445](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13100445/)

### Inferences (practical warning-sign table for this person)
| Signal | Threshold to act | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| 14-day trend loss | >1 %/wk = too fast; >0.7 %/wk under ~84 kg = too fast | Moderate (Garthe, Helms, Ruiz-Castellano) |
| e1RM on main lifts | Falling ≥2 consecutive weeks or ≥5 % below the cut's peak | Moderate (case data; relative strength should hold) |
| RHR | A *falling* RHR alongside fatigue or coldness = deep deficit; a sudden *rise* plus low HRV = illness or overreach | Weak (RHR falls in prep; HRV not studied) |
| Hair shedding | Noticeable increase 2–4 months after the fast phase (now) | Moderate for TE vs fast loss |
| Libido, morning erections, cold intolerance, lethargy | Persistent for >1–2 weeks | Moderate (T and T3 suppression in prep) |
| Hunger, food preoccupation, binge urges | Rising restraint or loss-of-control eating | Moderate (case data, Keys landmark) |
| Subjective sleep | Worse PSQI-type complaints before Garmin shows it | Weak to moderate |
| RUQ pain after fatty meals | Any → doctor | Strong (gallstone risk rises with fast loss) |

### Gaps
- No studies validated HRV (Garmin-style) as an early marker of "dieting too fast" in men. I found no evidence-based threshold for strength loss in non-competitors.

## Summary verdict on the current hard limits (for the report writer)
- **Max 1 %/wk (14-day trend)**: supported as an absolute ceiling. **Tighten to ≤0.7 %/wk now, and to ≤0.5 %/wk below ~18 % BF (~84 kg)** (Garthe 2011; Helms 2020; Ruiz-Castellano 2021; Forbes/Hall leanness effect).
- **≥1,700 kcal**: **too low for his training load** if read as total intake. Replace or augment it with an EA floor of **≥25 kcal/kg FFM/day net of exercise (≈1,700 kcal + exercise kcal)**. The 2,520 kcal now in place is consistent with this.
- **Protein ≥1.6 g/kg**: the evidence floor for a lean, trained person in a deficit is higher. **Prefer ≥2.0 g/kg (≈2.2–2.6 g/kg FFM)** as he gets leaner. The current ~1.9 g/kg is acceptable.
- **Fat ≥0.6 g/kg**: consistent with gallstone prevention (≥19–30 % of energy) and the 0.5 g/kg athletic minimum. It sits at the low edge, so ~0.7–0.8 g/kg would add margin for gallbladder, testosterone and skin.
- **Diet break after 10–12 weeks**: evidence is neutral to positive (MATADOR helps RMR in sedentary men with obesity; Peos is neutral in trained athletes). The current plan runs about 22 weeks to maintenance on 12-14, which exceeds his own rule. A 1–2 week maintenance block soon is defensible on adherence, mood and RMR grounds.
- **Dry or peeling skin and itch**: no direct rate-of-loss evidence. Check fat quality, zinc and ferritin via bloodwork (no supplements), and given HBV, mention the itch to his hepatologist.
